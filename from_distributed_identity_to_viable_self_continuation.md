# From Distributed Identity to Viable Self-Continuation: Typed Distinction Networks, Enabling and Constraining Supports, Commitment-Relative Corridors, and Reopenable Identity Architecture

## Abstract

A person is not preserved by holding one exact state.

A self-description is not an identity architecture.

A record may support a person, constrain a person, provide evidence about a person, or become necessary to a selected continuation without being the person itself.

A recurring distinction may organize many later judgments without being continuously conscious.

A commitment may remain authoritative while its implementation fails.

An institution may preserve an identity projection that the person rejects.

These differences become important once identity is treated as maintained continuation through a changing and distributed realization rather than as a fixed list of properties or a complete internal archive.

The relevant organization includes:

```text
body
memory
habit
skill
selective susceptibility
conceptual distinctions
commitments
relationships
tools
records
institutions
public projections
access paths
revision rights
maintenance and recovery processes
```

These components do not all play the same role.

Some preserve evidence.

Some make a capability available.

Some authorize a social continuation.

Some block or redirect reachable paths.

Some become bottlenecks through which many continuations must pass.

Some are externally controlled.

Some are replaceable.

Some are necessary only relative to one projection, role, contract, or horizon.

The phrase `topology of distinctions` can be made more precise by treating distinctions as nodes in a typed dependency network.

The relevant relations may include:

```text
activates
requires
constrains
overrides
reconstructs
suppresses
specializes
conflicts with
```

A distinction is high leverage not merely because many statements mention it, but because its loss changes many reachable judgments, actions, repair paths, or commitment-preserving continuations.

Identity can then be modeled as a **viable corridor** through a changing state space.

The corridor is not one exact biography, body state, public role, or psychological profile.

It is a region of trajectories within which selected capacities, distinctions, relationships, commitments, recognition paths, and recovery possibilities remain sufficiently available for a question and horizon.

This corridor may contain:

```text
learning
forgetting
role change
relationship turnover
reinterpretation
externalization
recommitment
recovery
intentional discontinuity
```

A person can change substantially while remaining within one selected corridor.

A person can also intentionally leave an inherited or harmful corridor and construct another.

The framework therefore distinguishes structural continuation from normative desirability.

A causal architecture explains which mechanisms preserve or constrain a continuation.

A normative architecture explains which commitments, obligations, rights, roles, or values make one continuation preferred, required, forbidden, or contestable.

The normative layer is not required for every descriptive identity question.

It becomes necessary whenever the analysis asks:

```text
which identity should be preserved
which external constraint is legitimate
which commitment may be revised
which record should remain authoritative
which discontinuity counts as betrayal, liberation, repair, or growth
```

The central proposal is:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{bounded embodied realization}\\
&+\;\text{typed identity-bearing capacities and distinctions}\\
&+\;\text{distributed enabling, witnessing, and constraining relations}\\
&+\;\text{commitment-relative corridor criteria}\\
&+\;\text{control, contest, substitution, and exit analysis}\\
&+\;\text{turnover, reconstruction, maintenance, and reentry}\\
&\longrightarrow\;\text{viable self-continuation without fixed-state identity.}
\end{aligned}
}
\]

---

## 1. Identity Is Neither One State nor One Archive

A static property model asks:

> Which properties does this person possess?

An archive model asks:

> Which records preserve the person's history?

A continuation model asks:

> Which realized organization keeps later interpretation, action, recognition, responsibility, and revision available as a continuation of this person?

The three questions are not equivalent.

A person may retain many properties while losing an essential capability.

An archive may preserve extensive history while no longer supporting any usable continuation.

A person may lose large amounts of autobiographical detail while preserving characteristic habits, commitments, relations, skills, and ways of discriminating situations.

Identity therefore cannot be reduced without loss to:

```text
a current state vector
an autobiographical memory store
a public profile
a legal identifier
a body description
a social reputation
a list of beliefs
```

Each may preserve an operative projection.

None alone determines every identity question.

The stronger claim is:

> Identity is a maintained family of possible continuations selected relative to a question, projection, contract, and horizon.

---

## 2. Identity-Bearing Organization

The phrase `identity-bearing disposition` can be useful at a coarse level, but it compresses several different architectures.

A person may preserve:

```text
a retained distinction
a practiced skill
a bodily habit
a selective susceptibility
a response threshold
a social commitment
a reconstruction procedure
a relationship-specific expectation
a prepared capability
an access path to an external provider
```

These should not be treated as one homogeneous kind.

A habit is not the same as a proposition.

A moral prohibition is not the same as an episodic memory.

A skill is not the same as a credential that authorizes its social use.

An external reminder is not the same as continuous internal retention.

A trusted friend is not the same as a public record.

The broader category should therefore be:

> **identity-bearing organization**: any maintained structure whose availability, transformation, or loss changes the selected family of personal continuations.

This category can include dispositions, but it does not make disposition the fundamental unit.

## 3. Identity-Bearing Capacities

Let:

\[
\mathcal K_P
\]

be the family of capacities available to person or continuing organization \(P\).

Examples include capacities to:

```text
discriminate
remember
recognize
interpret
act
refuse
promise
repair
relearn
reconstruct
coordinate
relate
contest
revise
```

A capability is not a free-floating adjective.

It depends on an architecture:

\[
\operatorname{Capable}(P,\kappa,C,H)
\]

only when the required mechanisms, resources, interfaces, environmental conditions, and maintenance paths remain available in context \(C\) over horizon \(H\).

The statement:

```text
I am this kind of person.
```

may describe a self-conception.

It does not by itself maintain the capacities associated with that description.

Likewise:

```text
I value reliability.
```

is not yet an implementation of reliable continuation.

The relevant architecture may require:

```text
memory
planning
reserve capacity
feedback
repair
social coordination
commitment tracking
```

Identity claims should therefore be separated from the generators that make them repeatedly operative.

---

## 4. Identity-Bearing Capacities Are Conditional

A person may possess a capability under one contract and lack it under another.

Examples:

```text
can navigate a familiar city
but not an unfamiliar city without maps

can maintain a relationship under ordinary distance
but not under complete communication loss

can perform a skill with available equipment
but not without the required interface

can preserve a commitment under moderate pressure
but not under every conceivable disturbance
```

A more precise capability claim is:

```text
Given context C,
resources R,
response horizon H,
and disturbance family F,
person P can preserve capability kappa
within bounds B.
```

This does not make identity arbitrary.

It makes identity claims testable and architecturally explicit.

## 5. From a Topology of Distinctions to a Typed Distinction Network

A person does not merely possess a set of isolated distinctions.

Distinctions participate in relations.

Let:

\[
G_D(P)
=
(\mathcal D_P,E_D,\tau_D,w_D),
\]

where:

```text
D_P   = distinctions reliably available to P
E_D   = relations among those distinctions
tau_D = relation types
w_D   = optional strength, frequency, latency, or consequence annotations
```

Possible relation types include:

```text
activates
    applying one distinction makes another relevant

requires
    one distinction cannot be competently applied without another

constrains
    one distinction narrows acceptable interpretations of another

overrides
    one distinction defeats a weaker continuation under declared conditions

reconstructs
    one retained generator supports recovery of another distinction

suppresses
    one distinction prevents another from becoming operative

specializes
    one distinction refines a broader equivalence class

conflicts with
    the simultaneous commitments supported by two distinctions do not compose
```

The phrase `topology` can remain as a broad descriptive term.

The typed network supplies the operational precision.

---

## 6. Why Typed Relations Matter

An untyped edge:

\[
d_i\rightarrow d_j
\]

is ambiguous.

It may mean:

```text
logical implication
causal activation
learning prerequisite
memory cue
normative priority
practical dependency
social association
mutual inhibition
```

These relations have different failure modes.

If \(d_i\) is a memory cue for \(d_j\), another cue may substitute.

If \(d_i\) is a logical prerequisite, substitution may require a different derivation.

If \(d_i\) is an institutional authorization condition, the person may understand \(d_j\) while remaining unable to act on it.

If \(d_i\) suppresses an impulsive continuation, weakening it may increase propagation rather than merely remove information.

Typed relations prevent the network from collapsing different kinds of continuation into one generic association.

## 7. High-Leverage Distinctions, Hubs, and Separators

A distinction may have large downstream reach.

Let:

\[
\operatorname{Reach}_{\tau}(d)
\]

be the family of later distinctions, judgments, actions, or repair paths reachable through selected relation types \(\tau\).

But reach alone is insufficient.

A distinction can be widely connected while easily replaceable.

A less frequently used distinction may be a bottleneck whose loss blocks every viable continuation of one important kind.

A leverage profile may therefore be represented as:

\[
\Lambda(d)
=
(
\operatorname{reach},
\operatorname{betweenness},
\operatorname{irreplaceability},
\operatorname{recovery\ latency},
\operatorname{failure\ consequence}
).
\]

A **distinction hub** organizes many transitions.

A **distinction separator** lies on every currently viable reconstruction or interpretation path for a selected question.

A **distinction family** may provide substitutable routes.

Let \(\mathcal P_D(a,b;C,H)\) be the viable distinction paths connecting operative condition \(a\) to later continuation \(b\).

A necessary distinction family \(\mathcal H_D\) satisfies:

\[
\forall p\in\mathcal P_D(a,b;C,H),
\qquad
p\cap\mathcal H_D\neq\varnothing.
\]

This permits high leverage to be analyzed as a path property rather than merely an impression of conceptual importance.

## 8. Four Different Relations to Identity

External and internal loci can relate to identity in different ways.

At least four roles should be distinguished.

### Witness or evidence

A locus preserves a projection that supports an identity claim.

Examples:

```text
photograph
message history
work product
institutional record
friend's memory
```

### Enabling support

A locus makes a capability, distinction, or continuation available.

Examples:

```text
calendar reminder
prosthetic device
accessible notes
trusted collaborator
credential needed for practice
```

### Constraining support

A locus narrows, blocks, conditions, or redirects reachable continuations.

Examples:

```text
legal status
platform account rules
medical classification
credit record
organizational role
```

### Constitutive dependency relative to a projection

A locus or locus family is necessary for every viable continuation under a selected identity question and horizon.

This does not make the locus an eternal metaphysical part of the person.

It means that, under the current architecture, no admissible path preserving the selected identity projection avoids that dependency.

The same object may occupy several roles at once.

A professional license may:

```text
provide evidence of qualification
enable legal practice
constrain permitted activity
be constitutively necessary for continuation in one regulated role
```

## 9. Constitutive Dependence Is Projection-Relative

Let:

\[
\mathcal V_{P,q,H}
\]

be the viable identity trajectories for person \(P\), question \(q\), and horizon \(H\).

A support family \(X\) is constitutively necessary relative to \((P,q,H)\) when:

\[
\forall \gamma\in\mathcal V_{P,q,H},
\qquad
\gamma\cap X\neq\varnothing.
\]

Examples:

```text
bodily continuity may be necessary for one biological identity question

a legal identifier may be necessary for one institutional continuation

a shared history may be necessary for one relationship identity

a trained capability may be necessary for one professional continuation
```

Another projection may not require the same support.

This avoids two opposite errors:

```text
every useful support is literally part of the person

no external support can ever be identity-constituting
```

The correct relation depends on the selected continuation and available substitutes.

## 10. Partial Witnesses Remain Partial

A witness preserves selected differences.

Let:

\[
\Phi_i(h)
\]

be witness \(i\)'s projection of realized history \(h\).

A photograph may preserve:

```text
visible presence
appearance
location cues
other visible participants
```

while omitting:

```text
motive
consent
representativeness
emotional significance
accuracy of caption
```

An institutional record may preserve:

```text
recognized name
status
credential
date
classification
```

while omitting:

```text
context
contest
informal contribution
later revision
self-understanding
```

A witness can be accurate within its projection and still be misleading when treated as complete.

A witness can also be false, stale, inaccessible, or authoritative beyond its epistemic quality.

This last condition is especially important.

A record may be poor evidence and still strongly constrain future paths.

## 11. Enabling Identity Supports

An enabling support expands or preserves reachable continuations.

Examples include:

```text
notes that preserve unfinished reasoning
calendars that preserve commitments
medication that preserves bodily capability
friends who restore shared context
repositories that preserve project history
credentials that open institutional paths
assistive tools that preserve communication
```

An enabling support succeeds only when:

```text
the support exists
the support is reachable
the representation is interpretable
the interface remains compatible
the result arrives before need
the support is sufficiently trusted
the support remains relevant to the selected projection
```

Existence without access is weak support.

Access without interpretation is weak support.

Interpretation after the response horizon may be operationally equivalent to absence.

---

## 12. Constraining Identity Supports

A support can preserve organization by limiting continuation.

This is already familiar in other domains:

```text
permissions prevent unauthorized transitions
barriers preserve separation
thresholds prevent noise propagation
contracts restrict invalid composition
```

Identity-support architectures contain analogous constraints.

Examples include:

```text
a promise constraining later action
an institutional role constraining authority
a legal record constraining eligibility
a medical record constraining treatment paths
a credit history constraining access to financing
a public profile constraining interpretation by others
a family expectation constraining accepted role change
```

A constraining support is not necessarily harmful.

Some constraints preserve commitments, trust, safety, coordination, and responsibility.

The relevant question is:

> Which continuations are blocked, by which authority, through which mechanism, under which contract, with which correction and exit paths?

---

## 13. Coercive Supports as a Strict Subtype

Dependence is not automatically coercion.

Constraint is not automatically coercion.

A support becomes coercive relative to person \(P\), question \(q\), and horizon \(H\) when several conditions combine:

```text
the support gates a necessary or near-necessary path
another locus controls its interpretation or update
refusal, disagreement, or noncompliance carries significant penalty
the person lacks a viable substitute within the response horizon
contest, correction, portability, or exit paths are inadequate
```

A schematic predicate is:

\[
\operatorname{Coercive}_{P,q,H}(x)
\]

when:

\[
\operatorname{Gates}(x)
\land
\operatorname{AsymmetricControl}(x)
\land
\operatorname{Sanctioned}(x)
\land
\neg\operatorname{ViableExit}_{P,q,H}(x).
\]

This definition is structural.

Whether the coercion is justified requires a normative analysis.

A legal prohibition, emergency restriction, and abusive social threat may share some structural features while differing normatively.

---

## 14. The Control Surface of an External Support

Externalization should be analyzed not only through access but through control.

For support \(x\), define a control profile:

\[
\chi_P(x)
=
(
\text{read},
\text{write},
\text{interpret},
\text{authenticate},
\text{correct},
\text{contest},
\text{export},
\text{delete},
\text{substitute},
\text{exit}
).
\]

Different loci may control different operations.

A person may be able to read a record but not correct it.

An institution may permit correction but retain interpretive authority.

A platform may permit export but make substitution practically costly.

A friend may preserve a memory while the person cannot delete or revise the friend's interpretation.

The practical effect of a support depends partly on this distribution of control.

An accessible support can still reduce autonomy when another locus controls the authoritative projection.

## 15. Identity Gates and Institutional Hubs

Some external identity supports function as hubs.

They connect one transition regime to another.

Examples include:

```text
passport
professional license
educational credential
security clearance
account identity
insurance classification
employment record
medical authorization
```

A passport is not merely a stored description.

It is a verification and authorization hub within a travel corridor.

A license is not merely evidence of skill.

It is a gate within a regulated professional corridor.

A platform account is not merely a profile.

It may be an access hub for communication, work, payment, or public reach.

Hubs increase capability while concentrating failure and control.

A hub contract should expose:

```text
assumptions
guarantees
resource requirements
authority
failure modes
maintenance conditions
contest procedures
substitution paths
validity horizon
```

## 16. Hub Cascades in Personal Continuation

One identity hub often depends on another.

Examples:

```text
professional role
→ credential
→ issuing institution
→ records system
→ identity verification
→ account access

medical treatment
→ diagnosis
→ medical record
→ insurance authorization
→ provider availability
→ transport

public communication
→ platform account
→ authentication
→ device access
→ network access
→ provider continuity
```

Externalization can therefore create dependency cascades.

A person may appear to possess one stable identity resource while actually depending on a chain of:

```text
accounts
formats
institutions
credentials
permissions
providers
interpreters
```

The correct stopping rule is not to expand every dependency indefinitely.

It is:

> Reopen the cascade until the exposed contract is sufficient for the current decision, risk, and horizon.

## 17. Social Co-Maintenance and Social Enclosure

Other people maintain parts of identity through:

```text
recognition
memory
expectation
role assignment
promise tracking
reputation
language
shared practice
```

This can preserve continuation after personal memory weakens.

It can also preserve an outdated or unwanted projection.

Let:

\[
\Pi_{\mathrm{self}}(P)
\]

be a self-maintained projection and:

\[
\Phi_Q(P)
\]

be community \(Q\)'s projection.

The projections may:

```text
correspond
partially overlap
conflict
change at different rates
gate different continuations
```

Social support becomes social enclosure when others maintain a narrow projection and use it to block legitimate revision, exit, or alternative continuation.

The same relation may be supportive at one horizon and constraining at another.

## 18. Self-Endorsed and Externally Operative Identity

A person may reject a classification that remains externally operative.

Examples include:

```text
an outdated public role
a disputed diagnosis
a stale professional profile
a legal classification
a family-assigned identity
a platform-generated category
```

This produces at least two distinct questions:

```text
Which identity does the person endorse?

Which identity projection currently controls external transitions?
```

The answers may differ.

An externally operative identity can affect:

```text
access
trust
risk assessment
eligibility
visibility
obligation
sanction
```

without becoming internally endorsed.

A complete identity architecture must therefore represent distributed authority, not only distributed memory.

## 19. Causal and Normative Architectures

A causal identity architecture asks:

> Which mechanisms preserve, alter, constrain, or reconstruct this continuation?

A normative identity architecture asks:

> Which continuations are required, permitted, prohibited, preferred, or contestable under declared commitments, roles, rights, agreements, and values?

The two layers should not be collapsed.

A person may be causally able to abandon a commitment while remaining normatively obligated.

A person may be normatively permitted to revise an identity while lacking the causal resources to make the revision socially operative.

An institution may be causally able to preserve a record without having legitimate authority to do so.

The architecture is therefore:

```text
normative specification
→ obligations, permissions, commitments, and authority claims

causal architecture
→ mechanisms, resources, interfaces, dependencies, and constraints

runtime realization
→ actual continuation under current conditions
```

A normative claim does not instantiate its implementation.

A causal capability does not by itself establish legitimacy.

---

## 20. When Is a Normative Layer Necessary?

A normative layer is not required to answer every descriptive question.

One can analyze:

```text
which records exist
which supports are reachable
which dependencies are shared
which capabilities can be reconstructed
which external projection gates a path
```

without deciding whether the arrangement is good.

A normative layer becomes necessary when the question includes terms such as:

```text
should
ought
authentic
legitimate
responsible
betrayal
liberation
harmful
fair
coercive in the morally evaluative sense
```

The structural architecture can identify:

```text
asymmetric control
unavoidable gating
sanctions
absence of exit
conflicting commitments
```

But whether these are justified depends on a declared normative contract.

## 21. Commitment-Relative Identity Maintenance

Let:

\[
\mathcal N_P
\]

represent the operative normative organization relevant to person \(P\).

It may include:

```text
self-endorsed commitments
promises
roles
agreements
obligations
rights
prohibitions
values
institutional authority claims
```

Not every element has equal authority.

Not every element is mutually compatible.

A commitment-relative identity analysis asks:

```text
which commitments remain authoritative
which are merely inherited
which conflict
which are revisable
which have external beneficiaries
which have implementation support
which lack a viable continuation
```

Identity maintenance is not simply preservation of everything once associated with the person.

It is selective continuation relative to an evolving commitment architecture.

## 22. A Commitment Is Not Its Implementation

The statement:

```text
I remain committed to X.
```

can be true while the architecture required to realize \(X\) is absent.

A commitment may require:

```text
time
attention
money
skill
permission
coordination
health
access
maintenance
```

The correct relation is:

```text
commitment
+
implemented mechanisms
+
resources
+
interfaces
+
compatible context
→
commitment-preserving continuation
```

This yields three distinct failures:

```text
normative abandonment
    the commitment is no longer authoritative

architectural failure
    the commitment remains authoritative but lacks support

runtime failure
    the architecture exists but fails under actual conditions
```

These should not be treated as the same identity change.

## 23. Identity as a Viable Corridor

Let:

\[
\Omega_P
\]

be a state space containing the variables relevant to the selected identity question.

These may include:

```text
bodily capability
memory availability
habit
skill
relationship state
social recognition
institutional status
external support access
commitment authority
control rights
resource reserves
```

Let:

\[
\operatorname{Traj}(\Omega_P,H)
\]

be trajectories over horizon \(H\).

Define the identity corridor:

\[
\mathcal V_{P,q,H}^{\mathcal N}
\subseteq
\operatorname{Traj}(\Omega_P,H)
\]

as the trajectories under which the capacities, distinctions, relations, commitments, access paths, and recovery possibilities required by question \(q\) remain sufficiently available under normative organization \(\mathcal N\).

This corridor is not one exact life path.

It is a maintained region of acceptable variation.

---

## 24. Corridor Adequacy

A trajectory may remain inside an identity corridor when:

```text
selected capabilities remain operative or recoverable before need

selected high-leverage distinctions remain available or reconstructable

selected commitments remain authoritative or are legitimately revised

necessary support paths remain reachable

external constraints remain within the declared contract

deviations remain detectable

repair, substitution, or reentry remains possible
```

A schematic adequacy predicate is:

\[
\operatorname{Adeq}_{P,q,\mathcal N}(s_t,C_t).
\]

Then:

\[
\gamma\in\mathcal V_{P,q,H}^{\mathcal N}
\]

when the trajectory satisfies the required adequacy conditions across the horizon, allowing declared degraded and recovery intervals.

Identity continuation therefore permits disturbance, turnover, and temporary degradation.

It does not require invariance at every instant.

---

## 25. Multiple Identity Corridors

A person participates in several partially overlapping corridors.

Examples include:

```text
bodily continuation
autobiographical continuation
legal identity
professional identity
relationship identity
moral commitment identity
intellectual identity
public identity
```

Let:

\[
\mathcal V^{(1)},\ldots,\mathcal V^{(k)}
\]

be these corridors.

Their intersection may be broad, narrow, or empty.

A professional continuation may require an institutional role that conflicts with a personal commitment.

A legal identity may remain stable while autobiographical memory changes.

A public identity may remain frozen while self-understanding changes.

A relationship identity may persist through changed roles while trust does not.

No single projection should be assumed to settle every identity question.

---

## 26. Preferred Interiors, Warning Regions, and Discontinuity

An identity corridor can be partitioned into regions:

\[
\mathcal N
\subset
\mathcal P
\subset
\mathcal W
\subset
\mathcal I
\subset
\mathcal R
\subset
\Omega_P,
\]

where:

```text
N = preferred or nominal interior
P = permitted variation
W = warning region
I = intervention or deliberate reconsideration region
R = recoverable or reconfigurable region
```

Outside the recoverable region lies discontinuity relative to the selected projection and current architecture.

These regions are not universal judgments about a person.

They are contract-relative descriptions.

For one commitment, forgetting a detail may be harmless.

For another, loss of a prohibition, promise, or emergency capability may require immediate intervention.

A boundary can also be crossed intentionally when the selected identity corridor itself should be revised.

---

## 27. Identity Change Is Not Automatically Corridor Failure

A changed role, belief, relationship, habit, or self-description does not automatically constitute identity loss.

Possible outcomes include:

```text
variation within the corridor

repair after disturbance

reconstruction through a different support path

legitimate revision of the corridor

transition into another corridor

loss of one projection while others continue

unrecoverable discontinuity relative to a selected question
```

The relevant distinction is not:

```text
changed
versus
unchanged
```

but:

```text
which continuation remains reachable
which contract still applies
which projection is preserved
which commitment has been revised
which repair paths remain open
```

---

## 28. Intentional Corridor Exit

Some identity corridors should not be preserved.

Examples may include:

```text
an imposed role
an obsolete professional identity
a harmful self-description
an inherited commitment no longer endorsed
a socially enforced projection
a dependency architecture that prevents revision
```

Leaving such a corridor may involve:

```text
ending commitments
changing relationships
contesting records
losing access
acquiring new capabilities
rebuilding social recognition
accepting temporary instability
```

From the old corridor's perspective, this may look like failure.

From the new commitment architecture's perspective, it may be necessary realization.

This is why structural continuity and normative desirability must remain distinct.

---

## 29. Selective Susceptibility and Identity Stability

A useful identity-bearing organization is neither completely rigid nor arbitrarily rewriteable.

It should remain stable under many ordinary disturbances while changing under a smaller family of organized interventions.

Examples include:

```text
learning from evidence
revising a false belief
ending an invalid commitment
updating an outdated record
recovering a forgotten skill
responding to a serious relationship change
```

Let:

\[
\mathcal D_{\mathrm{noise}}
\subset
\mathcal D_{\mathrm{update}}
\subset
\mathcal D_{\mathrm{destructive}}
\]

represent disturbance families that should respectively:

```text
leave selected organization invariant
produce controlled revision
destroy or disable the current organization
```

The classification depends on the selected corridor.

A robust identity is not one that never changes.

It is one whose susceptibility profile supports discriminating revision without uncontrolled propagation.

---

## 30. Disturbance, Propagation, and Identity Revision

An external event does not automatically rewrite identity.

The architecture is:

```text
disturbance
→ selective capture
→ interpretation
→ possible retention
→ propagation through distinction and support networks
→ possible commitment revision
→ changed continuation
```

A criticism may be heard without becoming authoritative.

A public record may become authoritative externally without being internally endorsed.

A traumatic event may alter bodily and affective organization before it is conceptually interpreted.

A repeated social projection may gradually constrain reachable roles.

Identity analysis should therefore distinguish:

```text
exposure
capture
state influence
operative discrimination
retention
authoritative commitment
social propagation
```

## 31. Finite Capacity and Identity Turnover

A finite person cannot indefinitely maintain:

```text
every memory
every capability
every relationship
every role
every public profile
every possible future option
every unresolved commitment
```

Let:

\[
B_{t+1}
=
B_t+I_t-O_t-R_t,
\]

where:

```text
B_t = occupied maintenance burden
I_t = newly admitted identity-relevant material
O_t = completed, externalized, delegated, or expressed burden
R_t = forgotten, retired, compressed, revised, or released burden
```

Sustained continuation requires turnover.

The design question is not whether turnover occurs.

It is:

> Which structures can be released without closing necessary continuations, and which losses remove high-leverage paths?

## 32. Forgetting, Retirement, and Selective Discontinuation

Forgetting can be:

```text
harmless detail loss
adaptive interference reduction
loss of evidence
loss of capability
release from an obsolete projection
failure of responsibility
```

Retiring a role can be:

```text
identity failure
normal turnover
necessary capacity release
moral revision
social conflict
```

The result depends on:

```text
what was lost
which dependencies relied on it
whether substitutes exist
whether obligations remain
whether affected others have claims
whether the corridor was itself still endorsed
```

Selective discontinuation is therefore part of identity architecture, not merely its breakdown.

---

## 33. Externalization Redistributes Burden and Authority

Externalization changes more than memory burden.

It can redistribute:

```text
storage
interpretation
verification
revision
authentication
authority
privacy risk
failure risk
```

A calendar can preserve a commitment while controlling when it reappears.

A medical record can preserve history while shaping future treatment.

A public profile can preserve visibility while narrowing interpretation.

A credential can preserve recognition while making continuation dependent on an institution.

The relevant burden vector is:

\[
\mathbf m(x)
=
(
 m_{\mathrm{attention}},
 m_{\mathrm{time}},
 m_{\mathrm{money}},
 m_{\mathrm{privacy}},
 m_{\mathrm{dependency}},
 m_{\mathrm{control}},
 m_{\mathrm{contest}}
).
\]

There is no universal minimum independent of the selected corridor and failure model.

## 34. Prepared Identity Capacity

Some capabilities must exist before the occasion of need.

Examples include:

```text
emergency contacts
access credentials
medical information
practiced skills
trusted relationships
recovery routines
portable records
recognized authority
```

Let capability \(\kappa\) require construction or relearning interval:

\[
L_\kappa.
\]

If the response deadline is \(T\) and:

\[
L_\kappa>T,
\]

then viable continuation requires one of:

```text
prior maintenance
reachable external provider
partial preparation
alternative capability
extended deadline
accepted failure
```

Prepared identity capacity is selective.

No finite person can preserve readiness for every possible future identity demand.

## 35. Reconstruction Is a Present Operation

A stored record does not contain a complete earlier identity waiting to be reloaded.

Reconstruction depends on the current person, current context, current interpretive scheme, and available witnesses.

A more adequate reconstruction relation is:

\[
R(d,P_t,C_t,W_t)
\longrightarrow
 d_t',
\]

where:

```text
d   = retained trace or generator
P_t = current person organization
C_t = current context
W_t = available witnesses and supports
d_t' = reconstructed operative distinction
```

The reconstructed distinction may correspond sufficiently to the earlier one for a selected purpose.

It need not be identical in every respect.

A journal entry may acquire new meaning.

A photograph may reactivate a changed relationship.

A concept may be reapplied with later knowledge.

Recovery is therefore path-dependent.

---

## 36. Recovery as Reentry, Not Return

After disruption, recovery may restore:

```text
routine
access
skill
relationship coordination
public standing
project context
bodily capability
commitment authority
```

Let \(\Pi_q\) be the selected projection.

Recovery may satisfy:

\[
\Pi_q(s_{\mathrm{after}})
=
\Pi_q(s_{\mathrm{before}})
\]

while:

\[
s_{\mathrm{after}}
\neq
s_{\mathrm{before}}.
\]

The person may return to work with changed expectations.

A relationship may resume with different trust.

A restored archive may lack metadata.

A corrected record may preserve chronology while changing authority relations.

Recovery constructs a new present inside a selected equivalence class.

---

## 37. Reentry May Use a Different Support Path

A robust corridor need not restore the same support arrangement.

It may use substitution:

```text
internal memory
→ external retrieval

lost credential
→ alternative verification

broken relationship support
→ another trusted witness

obsolete format
→ migrated representation

lost skill
→ relearning or delegated provider
```

Let \(p_1\) be the earlier support path and \(p_2\) the recovery path.

Identity recovery can succeed when:

\[
G_{p_2}
\models
A_{\mathrm{continuation}},
\]

without requiring:

\[
p_2=p_1.
\]

This makes portability and substitute hub families central to robust identity architecture.

---

## 38. Interior-Preserving Identity Maintenance

A maintained corridor should not require constant crisis-level correction.

Ordinary continuation can be shaped so that common actions remain inside a preferred interior.

Examples include:

```text
routines that preserve essential commitments
reminders that reactivate dormant distinctions
portable records that reduce provider lock-in
correction procedures for public data
reserve capacity for recovery
relationships that permit revision rather than freeze one projection
reversible experiments before irreversible commitment
```

This resembles anti-boundary design:

> Make ordinary, low-friction continuation compatible with the selected commitments and preserve intervention before destructive boundaries.

But the selected interior must itself remain contestable.

An institution can also use frictionless defaults to trap a person inside an externally chosen corridor.

Interior-preserving design is autonomy-supporting only when the person or legitimate shared authority can inspect, revise, and exit the corridor.

## 39. From Self-Description to an Identity Architecture Contract

An informal identity claim:

```text
I am dependable.
```

can be decomposed:

```text
Given:
    commitments remain visible
    required resources are available
    workload stays within capacity
    exceptions are communicated
    recovery paths exist

Guarantee:
    selected obligations are completed or renegotiated
    within the declared horizon and failure bounds
```

Another claim:

```text
This institution recognizes me correctly.
```

can be decomposed:

```text
Given:
    the record corresponds to the selected history
    correction procedures are reachable
    identity resolution is accurate
    interpretation rules are declared

Guarantee:
    the institution applies the selected status
    without relying on stale or mismatched projections
```

The goal is not to turn a person into a rigid specification.

It is to expose hidden dependencies when identity claims are being used to predict capability, responsibility, access, or continuation.

## 40. An Identity Architecture Compiler

A conceptual identity architecture checker could accept:

```text
Selected continuation:
    maintain professional practice for five years

Required capacities:
    domain skill, legal authorization, health, access to tools

Supports:
    employer account, license, records, collaborators

Constraints:
    renewal dates, workload, jurisdiction, privacy rules

Commitments:
    professional obligations, personal boundaries
```

Possible diagnostics:

```text
MISSING: no viable license-renewal path

WARNING: all work history depends on one inaccessible provider

CONFLICT: workload contract exceeds maintained health capacity

CONTROL RISK: external profile is authoritative but not contestable

RECOVERY GAP: no substitute authentication path

NORMATIVE CONFLICT: two commitments require incompatible continuations
```

The checker would not decide the person's values.

It would test whether the declared continuation is supported by the stated architecture and expose where normative choice remains necessary.

The same person can be modeled at different scales.

At one scale:

```text

→ role
→ institution
```

At another:

```text

→ habit
→ memory process
→ tool interaction
```

At another:

```text

→ verification service
→ eligibility decision
```

A locus is a question-relative analytical cut.

The cut determines:

```text

which state is retained

which failures are local
```

No single cut is privileged for every identity question.

The locus should be reopened when a hidden dependency changes the selected continuation.

## 42. A Minimal Formal Architecture

Let a viable identity architecture be:

\[
\mathfrak I
=
(P,V,E,\tau,S,\Psi,\Pi,\mathcal D,\mathcal K,\mathcal N,\mathcal M,\mathcal R,\chi,H),
\]

where:

```text
P   = selected person or continuing organization

V   = biological, technical, social, and institutional loci

E   = potential support, witness, constraint, and reconstruction relations

tau = relation types

S   = realized states of the loci

Psi = coupling transformations

Pi  = projections defining operative identity claims

D   = maintained distinctions and their typed dependency network

K   = identity-bearing capacities and their conditions

N   = commitments, roles, obligations, rights, and authority claims

M   = maintenance, verification, turnover, revision, and recovery processes

R   = resource, capacity, timing, and failure constraints

chi = access, control, contest, substitution, and exit profiles

H   = selected continuation horizon
```

The viable corridor is:

\[
\mathcal V_{P,q,H}^{\mathcal N}
\subseteq
\operatorname{Traj}(S,H).
\]

A trajectory belongs to the corridor when the selected identity contract remains adequate through ordinary operation, declared degradation, repair, legitimate revision, and reentry.

## 43. Support-Path Adequacy

A support path \(p\) is adequate when:

```text
the required locus exists

the path is reachable

the representation is interpretable

the authority is sufficient for the selected interface

the control and contest profile is acceptable

the result arrives before the response deadline

the path remains inside resource and failure bounds

the output composes with the next continuation
```

For composed paths:

\[
p_1:a\rightarrow h,
\qquad
p_2:h\rightarrow b,
\]

static plausibility requires:

\[
G_{p_1}\models A_{p_2}.
\]

Identity-support composition may additionally require compatibility in:

```text
name or identifier
role
permission
timing
trust
interpretation
commitment
privacy
maintenance horizon
```

Sharing the label `same person` is not sufficient to guarantee compositional compatibility.

## 45. Central Principles

### Identity-Bearing-Organization Principle

> Personal continuation depends on heterogeneous capacities, distinctions, habits, commitments, susceptibilities, relations, and reconstruction paths rather than on one homogeneous store of dispositions.

### Typed-Distinction-Network Principle

> A characteristic topology of distinctions becomes operationally useful when relations such as activation, requirement, constraint, override, reconstruction, suppression, specialization, and conflict are explicitly typed.

### Distinction-Hub Principle

> A distinction is high leverage when its availability changes many downstream continuations, especially when it lies on necessary interpretation, action, or recovery paths.

### Role-Differentiation Principle

> Evidence, enabling support, constraining support, and constitutive dependency are different relations and should not be collapsed into the statement that something is `part of identity`.

### Projection-Relative-Constitution Principle

> A locus may be constitutively necessary for one identity projection and horizon without being necessary for every identity question or being an eternal part of the person.

### Constraining-Support Principle

> Identity supports can preserve continuation by blocking, authorizing, redirecting, or conditioning reachable paths as well as by storing or restoring information.

### Coercive-Support Principle

> A constraining support becomes coercive when it gates a necessary continuation under asymmetric control, significant sanction, inadequate substitution, and insufficient contest or exit.

### Control-Surface Principle

> External support should be evaluated through rights to access, interpret, authenticate, correct, contest, export, delete, substitute, and exit, not through availability alone.

### Institutional-Hub Principle

> Credentials, records, accounts, and legal classifications may function as transition hubs whose authority and failure modes shape personal continuation.

### Social-Enclosure Principle

> Social co-maintenance supports identity when it preserves useful recognition and becomes enclosing when it blocks legitimate revision through an outdated or imposed projection.

### Distributed-Authority Principle

> A person's self-endorsed identity and the identity projections that externally control access or obligation may diverge.

### Causal–Normative-Separation Principle

> Causal architecture explains how a continuation is realized; normative architecture explains which continuations are required, permitted, prohibited, preferred, or contestable.

### Commitment-Implementation Principle

> A commitment can remain authoritative while its implementation architecture is absent or failing; normative abandonment, architectural failure, and runtime failure are different conditions.

### Identity-Corridor Principle

> Personal continuation is better represented as a region of viable trajectories than as one exact state, biography, role, or psychological profile.

### Multiple-Corridor Principle

> Bodily, autobiographical, legal, professional, relational, intellectual, and public identity corridors may overlap, conflict, or continue independently.

### Contract-Relative-Boundary Principle

> Warning, intervention, recovery, and discontinuity regions are relative to selected capabilities, commitments, projections, and horizons rather than universal measures of personhood.

### Intentional-Exit Principle

> Leaving an inherited, harmful, or obsolete identity corridor may be a successful continuation under a revised commitment architecture.

### Selective-Susceptibility Principle

> A viable identity remains stable under many ordinary disturbances while remaining revisable through a smaller family of organized interventions.

### Finite-Turnover Principle

> A finite person cannot indefinitely maintain every memory, capability, relation, role, and possible continuation; sustained identity requires selective release, compression, delegation, and revision.

### Prepared-Identity-Capacity Principle

> Capabilities whose construction or relearning exceeds the response horizon must be maintained in advance, externally reachable, or replaced by a viable substitute.

### Contextual-Reconstruction Principle

> Retrieval reconstructs an operative distinction through the current person, context, and witness arrangement rather than restoring a context-free earlier content.

### Reentry-Not-Return Principle

> Identity recovery constructs a new realized state inside a selected equivalence class and may use a different support path from the one previously maintained.

### Contestable-Interior Principle

> Ordinary continuation can be shaped toward a preferred interior, but the corridor remains autonomy-supporting only when its authority, defaults, and exit paths are themselves inspectable and revisable.

### Reopenability Principle

> Any compressed identity locus, support, role, or projection should be reopened when a hidden dependency, authority relation, or failure mode changes the selected continuation.

## 46. What This Framework Does Not Claim

The framework does not claim:

```text
that a person is merely a graph

that every disposition should be replaced by a formal capability type

that every external support is part of the person in the same sense

that every constraint is coercive

that every coercive structure is necessarily unjustified

that all legitimate obligations are self-chosen

that self-endorsed identity always overrides social or legal claims

that institutional records are accurate merely because they are authoritative

that authority implies evidence

that evidence implies authority

that one identity corridor settles every personal identity question

that all identity boundaries can be quantified precisely

that every change is healthy

that every continuity is desirable

that every harmful identity can be left without cost

that forgetting is always adaptive

that externalization always reduces burden

that more control by the person is sufficient for every social coordination problem

that a normative layer can be derived from causal structure alone

that commitments are valid merely because they are maintained

that identity recovery recreates the earlier realized state

that a public projection determines the person's complete identity

that embodied, affective, procedural, social, and conceptual continuities are interchangeable

that one analytical cut is privileged for every question
```

It claims:

```text
that identity-bearing organization is heterogeneous

that distinctions become more precise when their relations are typed

that supports can enable, witness, constrain, authorize, or become constitutively necessary relative to a projection

that control, contest, substitution, and exit are central properties of external identity supports

that constraining support should be distinguished from coercive support

that causal continuity and normative desirability are different questions

that personal continuation ordinarily occupies a corridor of trajectories rather than one exact state

that multiple identity corridors can overlap or conflict

that finite capacity requires turnover and selective discontinuity

that recovery is reconstruction and reentry rather than literal return

that identity architectures should remain reopenable when hidden dependencies or authority relations become relevant
```

## 47. Central Statements

> A person is not one exact state preserved through time.

> A self-description is not the architecture that makes the description repeatedly true.

> Identity-bearing organization includes distinctions, capacities, habits, commitments, susceptibilities, relations, and reconstruction paths.

> A topology of distinctions becomes more useful when its edges are typed.

> High leverage is a path property, not merely conceptual popularity.

> A photograph may be evidence without being support.

> A credential may be evidence, support, constraint, and a necessary hub at the same time.

> A record can be epistemically weak and institutionally powerful.

> Availability is not authority.

> Authority is not accuracy.

> Dependence is not automatically coercion.

> Coercion appears when necessary continuation is asymmetrically gated and viable contest or exit is absent.

> External memory redistributes not only storage burden but interpretation and control.

> Other people can preserve a person while also preserving an obsolete person-projection.

> Self-endorsed identity and externally operative identity can diverge.

> Causal architecture explains what can continue.

> Normative architecture explains what ought to continue under a declared commitment structure.

> A value statement does not implement the mechanisms required to live by that value.

> Personal identity is better modeled as a viable corridor than as one point.

> A corridor can contain learning, forgetting, role change, reinterpretation, and recommitment.

> Leaving one identity corridor may preserve a more deeply endorsed continuation.

> Stability requires selective susceptibility, not total resistance to change.

> Finite continuation requires turnover.

> Recovery reconstructs a supported present.

> Reentry can succeed through a different support path.

> A support architecture is robust only when its dependencies, authorities, failure modes, and exit paths are visible.

## 48. Conclusion

A finite person cannot preserve every earlier state, memory, capability, relationship, role, public description, and unrealized future with equal authority.

Identity therefore cannot be maintained by total retention.

It is maintained through selective organization.

Some distinctions remain internal.

Some are externalized.

Some are periodically reconstructed.

Some become embodied habits.

Some are preserved by other people.

Some are made authoritative by institutions.

Some supports open paths.

Some constrain paths.

Some become unavoidable hubs.

Some preserve evidence without preserving capability.

Some preserve capability without preserving autonomy.

The person continues not by remaining at one point, but by moving through a viable corridor.

That corridor is supported by:

```text
identity-bearing capacities
high-leverage distinctions
selective susceptibilities
commitments and obligations
relationships and witnesses
technical and institutional supports
control and contest rights
maintenance, turnover, and recovery paths
```

The corridor remains viable only when its supporting paths compose.

A remembered distinction may fail if it no longer guides action.

A commitment may fail if it lacks implementation.

A credential may fail if its institution disappears.

A public record may continue to constrain action after it becomes false.

A relationship may preserve history while preventing revision.

A support may become coercive when it is unavoidable, externally controlled, sanctioning, and effectively uncontestable.

The normative question cannot be eliminated.

But it should not be confused with the causal question.

The causal question is:

> Which mechanisms, resources, relations, and constraints make this continuation available?

The normative question is:

> Which continuation should remain authoritative, for whom, under which commitments and rights, and with which revision or exit possibilities?

The combined architecture is:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{embodied and social realization}\\
&\xrightarrow{\text{selective discrimination and susceptibility}}\\
\text{identity-bearing capacities and typed distinction networks}\\
&\xrightarrow{\text{enabling, witnessing, constraining, and authorizing relations}}\\
\text{distributed support and control architecture}\\
&\xrightarrow{\text{commitments, contracts, projections, and corridor criteria}}\\
\text{selected viable continuations}\\
&\xrightarrow{\text{maintenance, turnover, contest, substitution, and reentry}}\\
\text{continued or deliberately revised identity under finite capacity.}
\end{aligned}
}
\]

The practical question is therefore not:

> Which complete object is the self?

It is:

> Which capacities, distinctions, commitments, relations, and support paths must remain available for the selected continuation; which external projections enable or constrain that continuation; who controls their authority and revision; which dependencies are replaceable; which corridor is being preserved; and when should that corridor itself be left behind?
