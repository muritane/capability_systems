# From Role-Bound Identity to Viable Interaction Architecture: Protocol-Relative Equivalence Classes, Reachability Before Desire, and Reopenable Human–Agent Infrastructure

## Abstract

A road is not a vehicle.

A road network is not a route.

A reachable robot workspace is not a desired end-effector pose.

A collision-free trajectory is not an instruction to execute.

A protocol role is not the entity that temporarily occupies it.

A credential verifier need not know whether a request was produced by a human, a software agent, a robot, a browser, or a delegated service chain when those realizations satisfy the same interface contract.

These distinctions become important once organization is treated as the construction of viable interaction spaces rather than as the assignment of fixed identities to isolated entities.

The central problem is not merely:

> What does this entity want?

It is first:

> Which states, transitions, roles, interfaces, and continuations are actually available under the realized architecture?

Desire does not create reachability.

A target may be imaginable but absent from the current state space.

A destination may exist but remain disconnected from the available transport graph.

A route may be topologically present but unusable by the selected vehicle, agent, body, credential set, resource budget, or legal role.

A possible continuation after redesign is not the same as a currently executable continuation.

The relevant order is therefore:

```text
realized entities and environment
→ representation and schema
→ typed relations and interfaces
→ state and transition semantics
→ admissibility and viability conditions
→ visible reachable continuation space
→ candidate goals and routes
→ simulation and validation
→ execution, monitoring, repair, and replanning
```

This order applies across:

```text
transportation
robotics
software systems
human–computer interaction
law
institutions
markets
education
medicine
personal continuation
human–agent systems
```

The proposal developed here is that many apparently different domains instantiate a common organizational form.

They contain:

```text
entities or loci
protocols
role types
role instances
interfaces
credentials
permissions
prohibitions
resources
state transitions
transformations
verification procedures
failure boundaries
repair and exit paths
```

The entity occupying a role need not be permanently bound to it.

A computer may be a client in one relation and a server in another.

A person may be a teacher in one interaction and a student in another.

A physician may be a patient elsewhere.

A human may perform an API-mediated action today while a software agent performs the same action tomorrow.

The persistent structure is therefore often not the actor-role binding but the protocol-relative equivalence class under which different realizations are treated as operationally the same.

Let interface or protocol relation \(I\) be represented by a contract that observes only selected differences.

Two realizations are equivalent relative to that contract when both satisfy the same admissibility and guarantee conditions, even if their internal organization differs.

The framework therefore distinguishes:

```text
entity
role type
role instance
protocol relation
actor-role binding
operational equivalence
reachable continuation
selected trajectory
normative preference
execution
```

It also treats law, rights, privacy, consent, exclusion, invitation, recommendation, authentication, and institutional authority as parts of an interaction architecture that organizes otherwise excessive, chaotic, conflicting, or destructively composable possibilities.

Constraints are not merely subtractive.

They can create stable possibility spaces.

Road rules make transport coordination possible.

Grammar makes communication possible.

Protocols make distributed computation possible.

Permissions and prohibitions make durable cooperation, privacy, responsibility, and bodily security possible.

But every structural model is dual-use.

The same representation that enables diagnosis, repair, planning, and accessibility can expose bottlenecks, vulnerabilities, leverage points, and attack surfaces.

The relevant governance problem is therefore not whether structural analysis should exist, but:

> Who may inspect, modify, validate, contest, execute against, and exit the resulting architecture?

The central proposal is:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{shared physical and institutional world}\\
&+\;\text{typed entities, roles, interfaces, and transformations}\\
&+\;\text{protocol-relative equivalence and substitution}\\
&+\;\text{explicit feasibility, admissibility, and viability spaces}\\
&+\;\text{separation of possibility from preference}\\
&+\;\text{simulation, verification, governance, repair, and exit}\\
&\longrightarrow\;\text{reopenable interaction architectures for humans, agents, robots, and institutions.}
\end{aligned}
}
\]

---

## 1. The Primitive Problem Is Organized Interaction

A system containing several bodies, agents, resources, tools, records, and institutions admits many physically or technically possible interactions.

Without additional organization, one may be able to:

```text
drive almost anywhere the vehicle can physically enter
draw on any reachable surface
interrupt at any time
look at any exposed information
take any unguarded object
modify any writable state
address anyone in any manner
enter any unblocked space
consume any available resource
```

The problem is not simply that the number of interactions is large.

It is that unrestricted interaction does not reliably preserve:

```text
survival
coordination
privacy
ownership
trust
predictability
repairability
long-term planning
bodily integrity
resource continuity
role clarity
institutional memory
```

Organization therefore introduces distinctions among interactions.

It specifies:

```text
who may do what
to whom
through which interface
under which role
with which credential
at what time
under which conditions
with which consequences
subject to which correction or appeal
```

Law, protocol, etiquette, access control, scheduling, road design, and institutional procedure are different realizations of this broader problem.

---

## 2. Constraint Is Productive

A constraint does not merely remove freedom.

It can create a stable domain of meaningful action.

Without rules, there is not more chess.

There is no chess.

Without grammar, there is not simply more language.

There is reduced compositional reliability.

Without traffic organization, there is not merely more mobility.

There is increased collision, congestion, unpredictability, and exclusion of vulnerable participants.

The general pattern is:

\[
\text{unstructured possibility}
\xrightarrow{\text{typed constraints}}
\text{stable interaction space}.
\]

A useful architecture therefore does not maximize the number of raw transitions.

It constructs a region in which selected continuations remain viable.

---

## 3. Roads Are Not Vehicles

A road network is a support and constraint structure for transport.

It is not the vehicle.

It is not the driver.

It is not the destination.

It is not one route.

It defines a family of potential transitions.

A particular vehicle can use only a subset of them.

Let:

\[
G_T=(V_T,E_T)
\]

be a nominal transport graph.

The graph may include:

```text
roads
bridges
junctions
ferries
tunnels
restricted zones
seasonal connections
```

But the vehicle-relative viable graph is narrower:

\[
G_T^{(a,C,H)}\subseteq G_T,
\]

where:

```text
a = selected actor or vehicle configuration
C = current context
H = time horizon
```

A motorway may exist but be unusable by a pedestrian.

A dirt track may be usable by one vehicle and not another.

A ferry connection may be present but unavailable at the required time.

A route may be physically traversable but legally prohibited.

A road graph therefore represents potential structure, not universal reachability.

---

## 4. A Route Is One Trajectory Through a Possibility Structure

Let:

\[
\mathcal T(G_T,a,C,H)
\]

be the transport trajectories available to actor or vehicle configuration \(a\).

A route is one selected trajectory:

\[
\gamma\in\mathcal T(G_T,a,C,H).
\]

The route does not define the whole graph.

The graph does not choose the route.

The vehicle does not by itself determine the destination.

The correct decomposition is:

```text
vehicle and support capability
+
transport environment
+
constraints and current conditions
→
reachable transport continuations
```

followed by:

```text
reachable continuations
+
destination
+
preferences and costs
→
selected route
```

This separation is central.

---

## 5. Reachability Precedes Desire

The statement:

```text
I want to drive to X.
```

is not yet a route request that the current architecture can execute.

The target may be:

```text
absent from the model
fictional
physically inaccessible
outside the vehicle's medium
outside the available graph
reachable only after infrastructure redesign
reachable only under unacceptable risk
reachable only beyond the selected horizon
```

Atlantis, Antarctica, Mars, open water, air, and ice differ in their failure modes.

A destination may not exist in the represented world.

A destination may exist but lack a connected path.

A path may exist for another vehicle class.

A path may require a bridge, ferry, aircraft, launch vehicle, permit, support depot, or changed risk contract.

The relevant distinctions are:

\[
\text{imaginable}
\neq
\text{representable}
\neq
\text{reachable after redesign}
\neq
\text{currently executable}.
\]

A system should not force goal selection from an undifferentiated space containing impossible, unavailable, and executable continuations as though they were equivalent.

---

## 6. A Reachable Workspace Is Not a Desired Pose

Robot motion planning provides a precise version of the same distinction.

A workspace visualization may expose:

```text
reachable poses
unreachable poses
joint-limit boundaries
singular configurations
collision regions
low-clearance regions
alternative kinematic branches
```

Dragging an interactive marker can test whether a target pose is admitted by the current model.

It does not establish that the pose is desired.

It does not establish that executing the motion is appropriate.

It does not establish that the model is accurate.

The architecture separates:

\[
\text{reachable workspace}
\]

from:

\[
\text{candidate target}
\]

from:

\[
\text{validated trajectory}
\]

from:

\[
\text{execution command}.
\]

## 7. Planning APIs Presuppose a Representation

Collision checking does not occur in the abstract.

A planner requires an implemented representational substrate.

In a robotic system this may include:

```text
links
joints
joint limits
transforms
collision geometry
robot description
planning scene
attached objects
kinematic solvers
controller interfaces
```

URDF, Xacro, transform trees, meshes, semantic configuration, and solver-specific representations may participate.

Denavit–Hartenberg parameters may be used in some kinematic derivations, but they are not the universal representation of every planning system.

Only after the model exists do queries such as these become meaningful:

```text
Is this state valid?
Is this pose reachable?
Is this trajectory collision-free?
Can a path be planned between these configurations?
```

Without the representation, the API vocabulary is premature.

## 8. Representation Before Capability Planning

The same order applies outside robotics.

One should not begin with:

```text
Does the person possess the required capability?
```

before defining:

```text
what counts as the modeled entity
what a capability is
which components realize it
which interfaces expose it
which context variables matter
which evidence supports the claim
which substitutions are admitted
which failure conditions invalidate it
```

The correct order is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{domain}
\rightarrow
\text{representation}
\rightarrow
\text{composition}
\rightarrow
\text{state}
\rightarrow
\text{validity}
\rightarrow
\text{reachability}
\rightarrow
\text{planning API}.
}
\]

An architecture should derive its callable questions from its implementation and schema rather than inventing API-like predicates first.

---

## 9. The Human Domain Is Not Exempt from Structural Analysis

Humans are embodied physical systems participating in technical, social, and institutional environments.

They are not infinitely distant from ordinary physics, computation, control, representation, or interface composition.

Human systems are difficult because they involve:

```text
large coupled state spaces
partial observability
adaptation
learning
reflexivity
contested descriptions
institutional authority
normative disagreement
multiple time scales
heterogeneous realization
```

These are modeling complications.

They are not evidence that structural analysis is categorically inappropriate.

The relevant caution is not:

> Humans cannot be represented structurally.

It is:

> Every representation selects variables, projections, authority sources, and intervention points, and those selections must remain inspectable and contestable.

---

## 10. Robots, Humans, Agents, and Institutions Share a Physical and Causal World

A robot, human, software agent, and institution may differ radically in realization while participating in the same interaction architecture.

They may all:

```text
present credentials
request access
modify records
consume resources
occupy roles
send messages
receive permissions
trigger workflows
perform transformations
violate constraints
repair failures
```

The relevant question is not first:

```text
Is this actor human or machine?
```

It is:

```text
Which contract is being evaluated?
Which properties does the interface observe?
Which guarantees must the actor provide?
Which rights and liabilities are attached to the role?
```

Ontological differences may matter under some contracts.

They should not be presumed to matter under every contract.

## 11. Interfaces Often Observe Principals, Not Essences

A credential-verification service may evaluate:

```text
credential validity
issuer
scope
expiration
revocation
signature
requested operation
```

It may not represent whether the credential was presented through:

```text
a human at a terminal
a browser automation
a script
a software agent
a robot
a service account
a delegated process chain
```

At that interface, these realizations are substitutable when they satisfy the same contract.

Let \(I\) be an interface with observation projection \(\Pi_I\).

Two systems \(A\) and \(B\) are operationally equivalent relative to \(I\) when:

\[
A\equiv_I B
\]

if they provide the same contract-relevant behavior:

\[
\Pi_I(A)\sim_I\Pi_I(B).
\]

Internal equality is not required.

Interface-relative substitutability is sufficient.

---

## 12. Why Bots Can Exist on Almost Every Platform

Many platforms expose standardized action surfaces:

```text
submit text
click a control
call an endpoint
upload a file
read a response
maintain a session
present a credential
```

Any system capable of producing the accepted signals within timing, formatting, and authorization constraints can occupy the operational role.

A graphical interface may be biased toward human geometry through:

```text
screen layout
pointer motion
visual grouping
language conventions
human reaction-time assumptions
```

But the underlying transaction may still be actor-neutral.

The need for CAPTCHA, liveness checks, proof-of-personhood, anti-automation rules, and bot detection demonstrates that ordinary interfaces often do not inherently identify a human actor.

Human specificity is added through selected gates.

It is not automatically present in every protocol.

---

## 13. Substitution Is Projection-Relative

Operational equivalence is never universal.

A human and software agent may be substitutable for:

```text
retrieving a record
submitting a form
presenting a credential
monitoring a status
calling an API
scheduling an appointment
```

They may not be substitutable under a contract requiring:

```text
particular bodily presence
specific biometric identity
legal responsibility
valid personal consent
bearing a sanction
first-person testimony
```

But even these differences must be represented as contract requirements.

They should not be inferred from a general human–machine distinction.

Thus:

\[
A\equiv_I B
\not\Rightarrow
A\equiv_J B.
\]

Equivalence is interface-relative, role-relative, projection-relative, and horizon-relative.

## 14. Capability Is a Realized Path

A capability need not belong exclusively to one isolated entity.

The statement:

```text
Alice remembers the appointment.
```

may compress a distributed realization:

```text
Alice
+
calendar
+
phone
+
notification service
+
network
+
account credentials
```

Likewise:

```text
the agent booked the trip
```

may compress:

```text
model
+
runtime
+
browser
+
travel platform
+
credentials
+
payment service
+
human authorization
```

A capability can be defined as the existence of an adequate realization path:

\[
\operatorname{Capable}(A,\kappa,C,H)
\iff
\exists p\;\operatorname{Realizes}(p,\kappa,C,H).
\]

Different paths may mix biological, technical, social, and institutional components.

## 15. The Actor Is an Analytical Cut

Statements such as:

```text
the person did it
the robot did it
the institution decided
the agent booked it
```

may be useful compressions.

They select a locus as the actor.

The locus should be reopened when hidden dependencies affect:

```text
capability
responsibility
control
failure
privacy
substitution
authority
repair
```

The actor is therefore a question-relative analytical cut through a larger realization.

## 16. Protocol Before Role

A role does not exist meaningfully in isolation.

A client exists relative to a server under a protocol.

A teacher exists relative to students within an educational relation.

A doctor exists relative to patients, institutions, licensing, and medical procedure.

A buyer exists relative to a seller and transaction rules.

The relation is therefore more primitive than the isolated role label.

Let protocol \(R\) contain:

\[
R=(\mathcal R_R,\mathcal M_R,\mathcal C_R,\mathcal G_R),
\]

where:

```text
R_R = role types
M_R = admissible messages, actions, or transformations
C_R = constraints and preconditions
G_R = guarantees and postconditions
```

The protocol induces complementary or coordinated roles.

---

## 17. Role Types Are Not Permanent Identities

The sentence:

```text
Alice is a teacher.
```

compresses a temporary or context-dependent binding:

\[
\beta_t(\text{Alice})=\text{TeacherRole}
\]

under some protocol and horizon.

Alice may simultaneously instantiate:

```text
teacher
student
client
server
patient
parent
child
administrator
requester
provider
```

in different relations.

The role does not bind permanently to the entity.

The role type persists while occupants change.

---

## 18. Role Equivalence Classes

The phrase `equivalence class of roles` becomes precise when a protocol treats several concrete occupants as the same role type.

Let \(X_R\) be the set of concrete role instances under protocol \(R\).

Define:

\[
x\sim_R y
\]

when \(x\) and \(y\) satisfy the same role contract under the distinctions observed by \(R\).

Then:

\[
[x]_R
\]

is the protocol-relative equivalence class of role instances treated as operationally the same.

A human client, browser client, agent client, and service client may belong to one class relative to an API if they satisfy the same request contract.

They may fall into different classes under a legal, liability, or proof-of-personhood protocol.

---

## 19. Complementary Roles Are Not Equivalent Roles

Client and server are generally not equivalent under the same protocol.

They are complementary positions.

Every concrete client instance may belong to a Client equivalence class.

Every concrete server instance may belong to a Server equivalence class.

The protocol specifies how those classes compose.

The hierarchy is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{Protocol}
\rightarrow
\text{Role types}
\rightarrow
\text{Role-instance equivalence classes}
\rightarrow
\text{temporary actor bindings}.
}
\]

This avoids treating role labels as intrinsic properties of entities.

---

## 20. Role Switching and Relational Invariance

The same entity may occupy different roles at different times.

A machine may serve one request and initiate another.

A teacher may become a student.

A physician may become a patient.

A parent remains someone else's child.

The invariant is not the actor-role binding.

The invariant may be the protocol relation and its role semantics.

Let:

\[
\beta_t:E\rightarrow\mathcal R_R
\]

assign entities to protocol roles at time \(t\).

Role switching changes \(\beta_t\).

It need not change \(R\).

The protocol remains while participants exchange or compose positions.

---

## 21. Symmetry Is Not Sameness of Roles

A protocol can be asymmetric while remaining non-arbitrary.

Client and server may have different permissions.

Doctor and patient may have different responsibilities.

Teacher and student may have different authority.

Parent and child may have different obligations.

The important invariant is not equal action sets.

It is that role-specific rules are:

```text
explicit
publicly defined
consistently applied
contestable where appropriate
not silently rebound to favored individuals
```

A stronger symmetry condition is:

> Any entity occupying the same role class under equivalent conditions is evaluated by the same role contract.

This is protocol-relative role symmetry rather than equality of all roles.

## 22. Law as Interaction Architecture

Law can be analyzed as an architecture organizing embodied, informational, institutional, and resource interactions.

It specifies or influences:

```text
who may access which body
who may enter which space
who may inspect which information
who may create or alter which record
who may impose which obligation
who may exclude or invite
who may authenticate identity
who may recommend, rank, or classify
who may punish or sanction
who may appeal, correct, or exit
```

Law does not merely classify persons.

It creates, constrains, authorizes, and repairs interaction paths.

## 23. Rights as Constraints on Transitions

A right can be represented structurally as a constraint on what transitions another actor may validly perform.

For example:

```text
body access
+
absence of valid authorization
→
prohibited transition
```

or:

```text
private record
+
unauthorized disclosure
→
invalid information-flow transition
```

The normative source of the right is not reducible to mechanics.

But implementation requires an architecture that can:

```text
identify the protected locus
recognize the acting role
validate authorization
detect violations
apply remedies
support correction or appeal
```

Normative claims require causal and institutional implementation.

## 24. Privacy as Typed Information Flow

Privacy is not merely secrecy.

It includes control over:

```text
observation
collection
inference
storage
transmission
reuse
combination
retention
deletion
contest
```

A privacy architecture therefore requires typed information-flow relations.

The same datum may be:

```text
public for one purpose
restricted for another
shareable with one role
forbidden to another
valid for one horizon
stale at another
```

The question is not only whether information exists.

It is which protocol authorizes which transformation of it.

Bodily interaction is also organized through role, consent, context, and authority.

The physical possibility of touching, observing, restraining, treating, or modifying a body does not establish admissibility.

A bodily-access contract may depend on:

```text
consent

professional role
competence
necessity
proportionality
scope
time
withdrawal
recording
oversight
```

The body is not outside interaction architecture.

It is one of its most strongly protected and highly contested loci.

## 26. Recognition and Pronouns as Protocol Effects

Names, titles, pronouns, classifications, and public descriptions participate in social protocols.

They can affect:

```text
recognition

institutional continuation
```

A label is not only a descriptive token when institutions or communities attach transition rules to it.

The architectural question becomes:

> Under which protocol does this representation become authoritative, for which interaction, with which correction and contest procedures?

This avoids treating every dispute as either purely ontological or merely linguistic.

## 27. Inclusion, Exclusion, Invitation, and Recommendation

## 28. Infrastructure Defines Potential

Infrastructure does not need to choose a life, destination, task, or route.

Its primary function may be to construct and expose a stable possibility space.

Roads provide transport potential.

Networks provide communication potential.

Schools provide learning and certification potential.

Hospitals provide treatment potential.

Legal systems provide protected interaction potential.

Digital platforms provide communication, publication, trade, and coordination potential.

The architecture should make available continuations visible without confusing availability with desirability.

## 29. Possibility Is Not Preference

Three predicates must remain separate:

\[
\operatorname{Possible}(x),
\qquad
\operatorname{Admissible}(x),
\qquad
\operatorname{Preferred}(x).
\]

A continuation may be physically possible but prohibited.

It may be admissible but undesirable.

It may be desired but impossible.

It may be executable only after redesign.

A mature architecture exposes these differences rather than projecting them into one binary label.

## 30. A Continuation Workspace

Let \(\Omega\) be a represented state space for a selected domain.

Let:

\[
\operatorname{Traj}(\Omega,H)
\]

be possible trajectories over horizon \(H\).

Define a currently reachable workspace:

\[
\mathcal W_{A,C,H}
\subseteq
\operatorname{Traj}(\Omega,H),
\]

where \(A\) is the realized actor architecture and \(C\) the current context.

Define the viable continuation space:

\[
\mathcal V_{A,C,H}
\subseteq
\mathcal W_{A,C,H}
\]

as trajectories satisfying the declared admissibility, resource, safety, protocol, and recovery conditions.

A selected route is then:

\[
\gamma^*\in\mathcal V_{A,C,H}.
\]

The viable space precedes the selected trajectory.

---

## 31. The Workspace Should Show More Than Binary Feasibility

A useful representation should distinguish:

```text
currently executable
reachable through an available support
reachable after preparation
reachable only through a fragile bottleneck
reachable but not recoverable
reachable under disputed authority
reachable but prohibited
unknown because the model is incomplete
unreachable under the current architecture
reachable only after redesign
```

This resembles a robotic workspace with overlays for:

```text
collision
singularity
joint limits
clearance
uncertainty
alternative kinematic branches
```

For human–institutional systems, analogous overlays may include:

```text
resource saturation
credential gates
legal restrictions
institutional bottlenecks
privacy exposure
commitment conflict
reversibility
recovery basins
external control
```

## 32. Candidate Goals Should Be Queried Against the Workspace

Once the workspace exists, candidate targets can be tested.

Possible queries include:

```text
Is this target represented?
Is it reachable under the current architecture?
Which role bindings are required?
Which credentials must be presented?
Which resources saturate first?
Which constraints block the path?
Which substitutions exist?
Is the transition reversible?
What redesign would make it reachable?
```

These queries are meaningful only after the schema and state model exist.

---

## 33. Simulation Before Execution

A candidate continuation should be simulated or validated before irreversible execution when the cost of failure justifies it.

A general pipeline is:

```text
candidate target
→ target validity check
→ path generation
→ constraint validation
→ resource and timing validation
→ authority and credential validation
→ recovery analysis
→ execution authorization
→ monitored execution
→ replanning or rollback
```

This is not a claim that persons are robots.

It is a claim that reachability, validation, execution, monitoring, and repair are general organizational concerns.

## 34. A Common Agent–Environment Metamodel

A minimal cross-domain architecture may be represented as:

\[
\mathfrak A=
(V,E,\tau,S,T,O,U,P,K,R,G,H),
\]

where:

```text
V = entities, loci, or components
E = relations and interfaces
tau = relation and interface types
S = represented states
T = transition and transformation semantics
O = observation and projection functions
U = available actions or messages
P = protocols and role schemas
K = credentials, permissions, and constraints
R = resources, supports, and dependencies
G = governance, contest, repair, and exit procedures
H = selected horizon
```

This metamodel can be specialized for:

```text
robot
human
software agent
institution
vehicle
household
human–agent hybrid
```

The specialization should occur through concrete schemas rather than by presuming a categorical divide.

## 35. Typed Relations Are Necessary

A generic edge:

\[
x\rightarrow y
\]

is insufficient.

The relation may mean:

```text
transports
authenticates
authorizes
requires
enables
constrains
overrides
transforms
observes
owns
delegates
revokes
substitutes for
conflicts with
repairs
```

Different relation types support different queries, failure modes, and compositions.

A credential relation is not a physical connection.

A legal prohibition is not a kinematic constraint.

A recommendation edge is not a command.

Typed relations prevent conceptual collapse.

## 36. Composition Must Be Explicit

Two individually valid interfaces do not automatically compose.

Suppose:

\[
p_1:a\rightarrow h,
\qquad
p_2:h\rightarrow b.
\]

Composition requires more than a shared label \(h\).

It may require compatibility in:

```text
identifier
time
role
permission
format
trust
resource availability
privacy
liability
semantic interpretation
```

A general composition condition is:

\[
G_{p_1}\models A_{p_2},
\]

where the guarantees of \(p_1\) satisfy the assumptions of \(p_2\).

This applies to software interfaces, institutional workflows, transport transitions, and human support paths.

---

## 37. Credentials Attach Authority to Roles and Operations

A credential should not be treated as a permanent essence of its holder.

A credential may specify:

```text
issuer
subject or principal
role or scope
validity interval
presentation interface
verification procedure
delegation rules
revocation state
```

Its effect is protocol-relative.

The same credential may authorize one operation and remain irrelevant to another.

A human, agent, or service may present it if the protocol permits delegation or bearer use.

The architecture should expose who controls:

```text
issuance
storage
presentation
delegation
renewal
revocation
contest
```

## 38. Structural Knowledge Is Dual-Use

Every accurate structural model expands intervention capability.

Anatomy enables surgery and targeted injury.

A network model enables repair and attack.

A psychological model enables therapy and manipulation.

A social graph enables coordination and coercion.

A robot model enables planning and sabotage.

The general relation is:

\[
\text{better structural representation}
\Longrightarrow
\text{larger intervention space}.
\]

The representation itself does not select the objective.

---

## 39. A Model Is Both Maintenance Surface and Attack Surface

Publishing:

```text
kinematic chains
joint limits
collision geometry
controller interfaces
```

can show both how to operate a robot and where it is fragile.

Likewise, exposing:

```text
human dependencies
institutional bottlenecks
credential chains
social vulnerabilities
high-leverage distinctions
```

can support care, accessibility, continuity, exploitation, or control.

The relevant problem is not to forbid structural understanding categorically.

It is to govern model access and intervention rights.

---

## 40. Governance Is Part of the Architecture

For each model or support surface, ask who may:

```text
inspect
measure
infer
write
modify
validate
simulate
execute
contest
export
delete
substitute
exit
```

Let the governance profile of model \(m\) relative to actor \(a\) be:

\[
\chi_a(m)=
(
\text{read},
\text{write},
\text{interpret},
\text{validate},
\text{execute},
\text{contest},
\text{export},
\text{delete},
\text{exit}
).
\]

Structural transparency without governance can redistribute power asymmetrically.

Governance without sufficient representation can prevent repair and redesign.

## 41. Protection and Intervention Are in Tension

A system that cannot be inspected may resist manipulation.

It may also resist diagnosis, repair, accessibility support, and redesign.

A system that is fully observable may become easier to maintain.

It may also become easier to exploit.

The design problem is not absolute opacity versus absolute transparency.

It is selective observability and controlled intervention.

A viable architecture should preserve:

```text
repairability
contestability
privacy
least-privilege access
purpose limitation
reversibility
accountability
```

---

## 42. Human Rights as Governance of Interaction Surfaces

Human rights contests can be interpreted partly as disputes over the governance of interaction surfaces.

They ask:

```text
Who controls bodily access?
Which classifications may become authoritative?
Which properties are public, private, or protected?
Who may exclude, rank, recommend, or sanction?
Which identities may be recorded or revised?
Which speech acts create obligations or harm?
Which roles may be entered, refused, or exited?
```

These are not only debates about what people are.

They are debates about which interaction contracts institutions and other actors may impose.

---

## 43. The Architecture Need Not Encode Exact Preferences

Infrastructure cannot generally anticipate every individual's exact:

```text
preference
desire
wish
capability
risk tolerance
life plan
```

Its purpose is not to dictate a unique trajectory.

It should instead provide:

```text
stable options
legible constraints
role-neutral access where appropriate
explicit asymmetries where necessary
contest and correction
support for redesign
```

A road system need not know every destination before being useful.

A legal system need not prescribe every life plan before protecting bodily integrity and property.

A protocol need not know every message content before defining admissible exchange.

---

## 44. Approximate Symmetry Through Role Invariance

The relevant symmetry is not that every participant has identical powers.

It is that the same role contract applies to equivalent role occupants.

Let entities \(a\) and \(b\) instantiate the same role class under equivalent conditions:

\[
a\sim_R b.
\]

Then role invariance requires:

\[
\operatorname{Eval}_R(a)=\operatorname{Eval}_R(b)
\]

with respect to the distinctions declared by the protocol.

If \(a\) becomes a client and \(b\) becomes a server, they receive the rules of those roles.

If they later switch, the rules switch with the role rather than remaining bound to favored identities.

This is a strong basis for non-arbitrary organization.

---

## 45. Explicit Asymmetry Can Still Be Fair

A protocol may deliberately assign asymmetric rights and duties.

Examples include:

```text
judge and defendant
physician and patient
parent and child
teacher and student
administrator and user
server and client
```

Fairness need not require identical permissions.

It may require:

```text
justified role differences
consistent role assignment
scope limitation
oversight
appeal
role exit
prevention of arbitrary rebinding
```

The asymmetry should belong to the protocol and role semantics, not secretly to the identity of the favored occupant.

## 46. Roles May Become Identity-Bearing Without Becoming Essential

Long-term role occupation can shape:

```text
habit
skill
memory
social recognition
resource access
commitment
self-description
```

A role may therefore become identity-bearing.

But it remains useful to distinguish:

```text
role type
role instance
role occupancy history
role-shaped organization
person or entity
```

A person can leave a role while retaining some of its learned organization.

A role can continue with a new occupant.

An institution can preserve a role projection after the person rejects it.

## 47. Identity as a Projection of Interaction History

Identity can now be treated as one projection of a larger interaction architecture.

Let realized history be:

\[
h=(s_0,a_0,R_0,s_1,a_1,R_1,\ldots).
\]

An identity projection is:

\[
\Pi_q(h),
\]

where question \(q\) selects the differences that matter.

Possible projections include:

```text
bodily continuity
role continuity
credential continuity
relationship continuity
commitment continuity
public recognition
capability continuity
```

Identity is therefore not necessarily the foundation of the architecture.

It may be an interpretation of trajectories through protocol and support structures.

## 48. Viable Self-Continuation Becomes One Special Case

A person's viable corridor can be defined as the subset of interaction trajectories preserving selected identity-relevant projections.

Let:

\[
\mathcal V_{P,q,H}
\subseteq
\mathcal V_{A,C,H}
\]

be trajectories that preserve the continuation relevant to person \(P\), question \(q\), and horizon \(H\).

The broader interaction architecture determines which trajectories exist.

The identity projection determines which of those trajectories count as continuation for the selected question.

Thus:

```text
interaction architecture
→ reachable continuations
→ identity projection
→ identity-relevant corridor
```

rather than:

```text
fixed identity
→ all later organization.
```

## 49. Architecture Redesign Is Distinct from Route Selection

When a desired target is unreachable, at least three responses are possible:

```text
choose another reachable target
change the route within the current architecture
redesign the architecture
```

Redesign may involve:

```text
new infrastructure
new credentials
new role definitions
new support providers
changed legal rules
new interfaces
changed vehicle or body capability
additional resources
longer horizons
```

A planner should not silently treat redesign as though it were an already available path.

It should expose the dependency:

> This continuation becomes reachable only if these architectural changes are first realized.

## 50. Reopenability

Any compressed actor, role, credential, protocol, or infrastructure component should be reopened when hidden structure affects the question.

Reopen:

```text
the actor
when distributed realization changes responsibility

the role
when assignment or authority is disputed

the credential
when delegation or revocation matters

the protocol
when assumptions no longer compose

the model
when uncertainty or bias changes reachability

the law
when implementation blocks the declared right

the corridor
when redesign creates new continuations
```

Reopenability prevents abstractions from becoming unchallengeable identities.

## 52. Practical Construction Sequence

A disciplined implementation sequence is:

```text
1. Select the domain and analytical boundary.

2. Identify entities, loci, and environmental components.

3. Define protocol relations before assigning permanent role labels.

4. Define role types and role-instance equivalence conditions.

5. Define interfaces, messages, transformations, and credentials.

6. Define state variables, observability, provenance, and uncertainty.

7. Define transition, composition, and substitution semantics.

8. Define physical, technical, legal, and normative constraints separately.

9. Compute or approximate reachable and viable continuation spaces.

10. Visualize available, fragile, uncertain, prohibited, and redesign-dependent regions.

11. Query candidate goals against the represented workspace.

12. Simulate and validate candidate trajectories.

13. Execute with monitoring, rollback, repair, and replanning.

14. Reopen the model when hidden assumptions change the result.
```

## 54. Central Principles

### Organized-Interaction Principle

> Laws, protocols, interfaces, and constraints organize otherwise excessive, conflicting, or destructively composable interactions into stable domains of possible continuation.

### Productive-Constraint Principle

> Constraints do not merely remove possibilities; they can create the reliable structure within which meaningful action, cooperation, privacy, and planning become possible.

### Infrastructure-Potential Principle

> Infrastructure primarily defines supported and constrained possibilities rather than selecting one desired trajectory.

### Reachability-Before-Preference Principle

> Desired targets should be evaluated against represented and currently viable continuation spaces rather than treated as executable merely because they are imaginable.

### Representation-Before-Planning Principle

> Capability, collision, reachability, and planning queries are meaningful only after entities, states, interfaces, transformations, and constraints have been represented.

### Common-Architecture Principle

> Humans, robots, software agents, institutions, and hybrids may instantiate the same classes of role, interface, credential, transition, and support structures while differing in realization.

### Interface-Relative-Equivalence Principle

> Different systems are operationally equivalent when they satisfy the same interface contract under the distinctions observed by that interface.

### Projection-Relative-Substitution Principle

> Substitution between humans, agents, robots, and services is relative to the protocol, role, projection, and horizon rather than universally valid or invalid.

### Distributed-Capability Principle

> A capability may be realized by a path through biological, technical, social, and institutional components rather than residing inside one isolated actor.

### Protocol-Before-Role Principle

> Roles derive their meaning from interaction protocols; they should not be treated as intrinsic properties independent of complementary positions and rules.

### Role-Class Principle

> Concrete occupants of a role form a protocol-relative equivalence class when they satisfy the same declared role contract.

### Temporary-Binding Principle

> Entities may enter, leave, exchange, suspend, and compose roles without becoming permanently identical to those roles.

### Role-Invariance Principle

> Equivalent occupants under equivalent conditions should be evaluated by the same role contract, even when the protocol assigns different powers to different role classes.

### Explicit-Asymmetry Principle

> Asymmetric roles can be legitimate when the asymmetry is declared, scoped, justified, consistently applied, and subject to appropriate oversight and exit.

### Law-as-Architecture Principle

> Legal systems organize bodily, informational, institutional, and resource interactions by defining permissions, prohibitions, authorities, remedies, and role transitions.

### Rights-Implementation Principle

> A right becomes operational only when an architecture identifies protected loci, validates authority, blocks or detects invalid transitions, and supports remedy or contest.

### Possibility–Admissibility–Preference Separation Principle

> Physical or technical possibility, protocol admissibility, and normative or personal preference are different predicates and should not be collapsed.

### Continuation-Workspace Principle

> A viable architecture should expose reachable, fragile, prohibited, uncertain, and redesign-dependent continuation regions before selecting a trajectory.

### Simulation-Before-Execution Principle

> Candidate continuations should be validated against state, resource, authority, timing, and recovery constraints before irreversible execution when failure costs justify it.

### Dual-Use-Representation Principle

> Structural knowledge expands both constructive and destructive intervention spaces; model access and execution rights therefore require governance.

### Selective-Transparency Principle

> Repairability and redesign require sufficient observability, while safety and autonomy require controlled access, purpose limitation, and contestable interpretation.

### Identity-as-Projection Principle

> Identity is one possible projection of trajectories through interaction architectures rather than necessarily the primitive from which those architectures are derived.

### Redesign-Distinction Principle

> A continuation reachable after infrastructure, capability, role, or legal redesign is not yet part of the currently executable continuation space.

### Reopenability Principle

> Any compressed actor, role, protocol, model, or credential should be reopened when hidden realization, authority, or failure structure changes the answer.

## 55. What This Framework Does Not Claim

The framework does not claim:

```text
that humans and robots are identical

that every human capability is currently machine-substitutable

that every interface should ignore human or legal distinctions

that every role is freely interchangeable

that all asymmetry is illegitimate

that law is reducible to software

that normative legitimacy can be derived from physical feasibility

that every desired target should become reachable

that all structural knowledge should be public

that privacy should prevent all modeling

that representation is ever complete

that every interaction can be formalized with equal precision

that operational equivalence implies moral equivalence

that protocol compliance exhausts responsibility

that identity is irrelevant

that infrastructure should choose individual goals

that constraints are always beneficial

that role invariance is automatically achieved by declaring a protocol

that a viable continuation space can be computed exactly in every domain
```

It claims:

```text
that organized interaction is a common structural problem

that roads, workspaces, protocols, and laws define potential spaces rather than particular trajectories

that planning requires representation

that humans, agents, robots, and institutions can share interfaces and role contracts

that substitutability is already widespread and interface-relative

that roles are protocol positions rather than permanent essences

that equivalent role occupants should be evaluated by role rules rather than arbitrary identity bindings

that legal and social systems already govern access, information, exclusion, recognition, and bodily interaction

that possibility, admissibility, preference, redesign, and execution should remain separate

that structural knowledge is dual-use

that model governance is part of the architecture

that identity corridors can be reconstructed as projections of a more general interaction architecture
```

## 56. Central Statements

> A road is not a vehicle.

> A road network is not a route.

> A reachable workspace is not a desired pose.

> A collision-free trajectory is not an execution command.

> Desire does not create reachability.

> A planning API presupposes a representation.

> A capability may be a distributed realization path.

> The actor is often a compressed analytical cut.

> Humans, agents, robots, and services can satisfy the same interface contract.

> Bot detection exists partly because ordinary interfaces often do not inherently distinguish humans from agents.

> Substitution is protocol-relative, not universal.

> A role is a position in a relation, not an eternal property of its occupant.

> Client and server are complementary role classes, not permanent identities.

> The same entity may serve in one relation and request in another.

> Equivalent role occupants should inherit the same role contract.

> Fairness need not mean identical roles; it can mean non-arbitrary role binding and explicit asymmetry.

> Law organizes transitions among bodies, information, resources, institutions, and roles.

> Rights require implementation through interaction constraints, verification, remedy, and contest.

> Infrastructure defines possibility spaces without selecting one particular route.

> Possibility is not admissibility.

> Admissibility is not preference.

> Preference is not execution.

> Reachability after redesign is not current executability.

> Structural knowledge is both a maintenance surface and an attack surface.

> Model governance is therefore part of system design.

> Identity may be a projection of trajectories through protocol and support architectures.

> The architecture should remain reopenable when hidden assumptions become consequential.

## 57. Conclusion

A stable world of interaction does not arise merely because bodies, agents, tools, and resources exist together.

Physical possibility permits too many conflicting, destructive, ambiguous, or non-composing interactions.

Organization introduces structure.

It creates:

```text
roads rather than arbitrary vehicle motion
interfaces rather than arbitrary state mutation
protocols rather than arbitrary message exchange
roles rather than permanent actor essences
permissions rather than unrestricted access
rights rather than unprotected exposure
repair paths rather than irreversible failure
```

The resulting architecture does not need to determine one correct route, life, identity, or goal.

Its prior task is to make the viable possibility space legible.

A transport graph shows where a vehicle may travel.

A robot model shows which poses and trajectories may be executable.

A protocol shows which role-relative exchanges compose.

A legal system shows which interactions are authorized, prohibited, protected, contestable, or repairable.

A human–agent infrastructure shows which capabilities can be realized by which mixtures of bodies, tools, credentials, institutions, and software agents.

The central sequence is:

\[
\boxed{
\begin{aligned}
&\text{world and participating loci}\\
&\xrightarrow{\text{representation}}\
\text{typed entities, roles, interfaces, and states}\\
&\xrightarrow{\text{protocol and constraint semantics}}\
\text{reachable and admissible continuation spaces}\\
&\xrightarrow{\text{goals, values, and preferences}}\
\text{candidate trajectories}\\
&\xrightarrow{\text{simulation and validation}}\
\text{executable plans}\\
&\xrightarrow{\text{monitoring, governance, repair, and redesign}}\
\text{continued organized interaction.}
\end{aligned}
}
\]

The practical question is therefore not only:

> Who or what is this entity?

It is:

> Which protocol is active; which role class is being instantiated; which distinctions does the interface observe; which human, agent, robot, service, or institution can satisfy the role contract; which states and transitions are currently reachable; which are merely imaginable or redesign-dependent; which constraints preserve viable coexistence; who governs the model and its intervention surface; and how can the architecture remain inspectable, contestable, repairable, and open to legitimate reconfiguration?
