# Maintained Loci of Constrained Realization: A Distributed Ontology of Capability, Infrastructure, and Organizational Persistence

## Abstract

A capability framework may begin with apparently separate concepts:

```text
requirements
provisions
interfaces
realization
composition
infrastructure
bounded agents
```

These concepts become difficult to connect when they are treated as independent primitives.

A requirement does not exist merely because an object lacks something.

A provision does not exist merely because an object possesses a resource.

An interface is not useful without a selected relation across its boundary.

Infrastructure is not maintained without some target, authority, viability condition, or historically stabilized concern.

The missing center is a distributed class of organized systems that preserve selected regions of realizability through time.

This document proposes the following central object:

> A maintained locus of constrained realization is an addressable and partially bounded organization whose state, dependencies, interfaces, transformations, observations, and maintenance processes jointly preserve or produce a selected class of realizable outcomes.

A locus may be:

```text
a cell
a person
a machine
a warehouse
a software service
a court
a road network
a compiler pass
a laboratory
a circulatory subsystem
a company
a protocol ecosystem
```

The locus is not necessarily spatially compact, centrally controlled, conscious, or materially permanent.

It is identified by the persistence of a selected organization and its relations across change.

The proposed ontology is therefore not centered on one whole organization.

It is distributed and recursively compositional.

A large organization may be modeled as one locus at one level and as a network of interacting loci at another. A component may simultaneously consume upstream capabilities, provide downstream capabilities, maintain an interface, generate evidence, interpret requirements, and participate in the preservation of a larger viable region.

This framework connects:

```text
identity
addressability
target regions
invariants
requirements
provisions
interfaces
bridges
composition
realization
maintenance
infrastructure
evidence
bounded invocation
failure propagation
provenance
reverse tracing
```

The central claim is:

> Capabilities, requirements, provisions, interfaces, and infrastructures are not independent substances. They are relational projections of maintained loci participating in distributed realization networks.

A second claim follows:

> Infrastructure is never neutral in the strong sense. It preserves a historically selected region of reachability for some agents, targets, observers, authorities, and conditions rather than all possible transitions equally.

A third claim concerns failure:

> When provision stops, material objects may remain, but reachable organization, composability, reproducibility, and future option sets can disappear.

A fourth claim concerns investigation:

> Realization networks can be reverse traced by following requirement, provision, interface, evidence, maintenance, authority, and provenance references, although no unique historical source is guaranteed.

The resulting framework is a preliminary ontology and mathematics of distributed maintenance: how selected possibilities are made reachable, kept reachable, exposed through small interfaces, inherited by bounded agents, and lost when recursive support fails.

---

## 1. The Disconnection Problem

Consider the following list:

```text
capability
interface
realization
composition
infrastructure
bounded agent
```

Each term appears meaningful.

Together they still feel incomplete.

The reason is that the list begins after a prior selection has already occurred.

It does not yet explain:

```text
why one outcome matters
why one capability is required
why one provision is maintained
who may declare a requirement binding
which observations define success
whose access the interface supports
whose costs remain outside the boundary
why one composition is preserved and another abandoned
```

Without these relations, the framework can describe how a network operates after its requirements have been established, but not why this network exists rather than another.

A neutral-looking chain such as:

```text
requirement
->
provider
->
interface
->
realization
```

conceals at least four prior selections:

```text
a target region
a relevant observer
a source of authority
a time horizon
```

The missing problem is therefore not another technical component.

It is orientation.

A realization network is oriented toward some selected class of states, transitions, obligations, or continuities.

---

## 2. Objects Do Not Intrinsically Require or Provide

An object does not usually require or provide a capability in isolation.

A stone does not simply provide weight.

It may provide usable weight relative to:

```text
a system that can locate it
a process that can move it
a context in which its mass matters
a structure capable of supporting it
an observer who distinguishes success from failure
```

A battery does not simply provide power.

It provides an admissible electrical capability only relative to:

```text
voltage
current
connector
charge state
temperature
access
safety conditions
time horizon
consumer compatibility
```

Likewise, an organism does not abstractly require oxygen.

A particular maintained physiological organization requires oxygen delivery within a range, distribution pattern, and time horizon sufficient to preserve selected viable states.

The stronger relational form is:

```text
organization O
in state x
under context c
with protected region V
and horizon H
treats capability kappa
as an open requirement.
```

Requirement and provision are therefore indexed relations.

They are not permanent metaphysical properties attached to objects.

## 3. The Candidate Unifying Object

The proposed unifying object is:


a maintained locus of constrained realization.

Call such a locus:

\[
\ell.
\]

A locus is not merely an object.

It is a persistent, addressable organization of state and relations that can:

```text
receive conditions
bind provisions
transform state
expose capabilities
produce evidence
maintain selected invariants
participate in larger compositions
```

A locus may be materially concentrated, such as a heart.

It may be geographically distributed, such as a logistics network.

It may be institutionally distributed, such as a legal identity system.

It may be computationally distributed, such as a replicated service.

It may be temporally distributed, such as a standard maintained across generations of implementations.

The locus is therefore not defined by spatial enclosure alone.

It is defined by a sufficiently persistent pattern of coordinated realization.

---

## 4. Why Not Merely `Organization`?

The word `organization` is close but too broad.

A frozen arrangement may be organized without currently preserving any relevant path.

A historical ruin may retain visible structure while no longer supporting the functions for which it was constructed.

A disconnected database may preserve data organization while losing operational addressability.

A machine may remain materially intact while lacking power, authorization, calibration, or maintenance.

The stronger concept must include:

```text
organization
+
current admissibility
+
maintenance
+
selected realization relations
```

Thus:

> A maintained locus is not only arranged. It remains capable of participating in selected realizations under declared conditions.

---

## 5. Why Not Merely `Capability`?

Capability is also too narrow to serve as the sole primitive.

A capability is always capability-for-something under some conditions.

It normally omits:

```text
who can access it
who maintains it
which interface exposes it
what upstream dependencies support it
what target makes it relevant
what evidence establishes it
how long it is expected to remain available
```

Capability is better understood as a projection of a locus boundary.

A locus may expose a capability without revealing its full internal organization.

Formally:

\[
\operatorname{Expose}(\ell,\iota,c,t)
=
P,
\]

where:

```text
ell = locus
iota = interface
c = context
t = time
P = set of provision claims
```

Capability is therefore not the whole locus.

It is one observable relation through which the locus can participate in further realization.

---

## 6. Why Not Merely a Dependency Graph?

A dependency graph captures which nodes rely on which others.

But a static graph may omit:

```text
state
capacity
timing
quality
maintenance
authority
observation
failure semantics
substitution
learning
historical formation
```

Two networks may have the same graph topology while differing radically in whether they are realizable.

One edge may be:

```text
currently witnessed
```

another:

```text
merely assumed
```

another:

```text
authorized but unavailable
```

another:

```text
physically possible but institutionally prohibited
```

The ontology therefore requires a dynamic, typed, evidenced, contextual graph rather than a bare dependency graph.

## 7. The Distributed Answer

The framework is not fundamentally centered on one whole organization.

It is distributed.

A whole organization is one possible analytical cut through a network of loci.

At one scale:

```text
hospital
```

may be treated as one locus.

At another scale it decomposes into:

```text
wards
laboratories
pharmacy
power systems
identity systems
staff roles
supply chains
databases
regulatory processes
```

Each of these may be treated as a locus with its own:

```text
identity
state
requirements
provisions
interfaces
maintenance
evidence
failure modes
```

There is no automatically privileged level.

The analytical boundary is selected according to the question being asked.

The same structure may therefore be represented as:

```text
one locus
```

or:

```text
a network of loci
```

without contradiction.

## 8. Recursive Local Completeness

A locus need not contain every condition required for its own continued existence.

It is locally complete only in a limited sense.

It contains enough organization to:

```text
recognize or instantiate local requirements
bind some incoming provisions
perform local transformations
expose outgoing provisions
produce local observations
participate in maintenance
```

Its upstream requirements may remain delegated.

Thus a locus may be represented as:

```text
incoming bound capabilities
+
local organization and state
+
selected transformation policy
->
changed state
+
outgoing capabilities
+
evidence
+
new requirements
```

The node is locally operable but recursively dependent.

\[
\mathcal L_\ell
=
(

B_\ell,
X_\ell,
\Omega_\ell,
\Sigma_\ell,
R_\ell,
P_\ell,
\mathcal I_\ell,
\Pi_\ell,
M_\ell,
E_\ell
).
\]

Where:

```text
I_ell       = identity structure
B_ell       = boundary or analytical cut
X_ell       = local state space and current state
Omega_ell   = retained internal organization
Sigma_ell   = selection regime
R_ell       = open capability requirements
P_ell       = exposed capability provisions
I_ell^if    = interfaces and access points
Pi_ell      = transformation and control policies
M_ell       = maintenance and recovery processes
E_ell       = evidence and provenance state
```

The selection regime is itself structured:

\[
\Sigma_\ell
=
(V_\ell,\Gamma_\ell,A_\ell,H_\ell,J_\ell).
\]

Where:

```text
V_ell       = target, viable, or protected state region
Gamma_ell   = observation and acceptance relation
A_ell       = authority or selection source
H_ell       = relevant horizon
J_ell       = preference, cost, or priority functional
```

This selection regime supplies the orientation missing from an isolated capability graph.

## 10. Identity

Identity allows a locus to be referred to across state change.

Identity is not identical to current state.

A hospital can remain the same hospital while:

```text
staff change
inventory changes
software changes
buildings are renovated
policies change
patients change
```

A service can retain functional identity while:

```text
instances restart
machines are replaced
IP addresses change
versions are upgraded
```

A useful identity structure may contain:

\[
I_\ell
=
(
\operatorname{id},
\operatorname{type},
\operatorname{scope},
\operatorname{version},
\operatorname{lineage}
).
\]

Identity supports claims such as:

\[
I_\ell(t_0)=I_\ell(t_1)
\]

while:

\[
x_\ell(t_0)\neq x_\ell(t_1).
\]

Identity may be:

```text
material
functional
legal
organizational
representational
historical
```

These identity criteria need not coincide.

A replaced organ may preserve system function without preserving material identity.

A renamed institution may preserve legal continuity while changing public representation.

A forked software project may preserve code lineage while losing organizational continuity.

The framework should therefore state which identity relation matters for each analysis.

## 11. Addressability

Identity does not guarantee addressability.

A locus may exist but be impossible for a selected consumer to locate or invoke.

Let:

\[
\operatorname{Addr}:D\rightharpoonup \mathcal A
\]

map entities or loci in a declared domain \(D\) to addresses in an address space \(\mathcal A\).

Examples include:

```text
memory address
process identifier
network endpoint
postal address
SKU
patient identifier
atom index
legal registration
role name
coordinate
interface name
```

But addressability is still weaker than accessibility.

Distinguish:

```text
identity
addressability
reachability
authorization
usability
```

A provider may be:

```text
identified but not addressable
addressable but not reachable
reachable but unauthorized
authorized but incompatible
compatible but unavailable
available but insufficient
```

Operational access is therefore a relation:

\[
\operatorname{Accessible}
(u,\ell,\iota,c,t),
\]

not a property of the provider alone.

## 12. Boundary

A locus requires a boundary, but the boundary is not necessarily a wall.

It is an analytical and operational distinction between:

```text
locally retained organization
and
externally delegated conditions
```

The boundary determines:

```text
what is modeled internally
what is represented as a requirement
what is exposed as a provision
what observations are visible
what costs are externalized
what failures are considered local
```

A boundary may be:

```text
physical
software-defined
legal
institutional
conceptual
temporal
contractual
```

Boundary selection is not neutral.

Moving the boundary can convert:

```text
an internal process into an external dependency
an external cost into an internal obligation
a hidden failure into an observable contract violation
```

Thus:

> A system boundary is a decision-relative cut through a distributed realization network.

## 13. Concern Without Anthropomorphism

The question `who cares?` is essential but should not force every locus to be conscious.

At least four forms of orientation should be distinguished.

### Experienced concern

A conscious agent experiences preference, pain, desire, fear, commitment, or intention.

### Functional viability

An organism or organization ceases to persist if some condition leaves a viable region.

### Delegated objective

A controller, employee, service, or institution acts under an objective selected elsewhere.

### Historical selection

A structure persists because earlier evolutionary, market, political, technical, or institutional processes stabilized it.

The framework can represent these through a general selection regime without claiming that all loci literally care.

The central question becomes:

> Which process makes some state distinctions operative for this locus?

## 14. Target Regions and Protected Invariants

Let \(X_\ell\) be the state space of locus \(\ell\).

A target or viable region is:

\[
V_\ell\subseteq X_\ell.
\]

A point target may be too restrictive.

Most systems preserve ranges:

```text
temperature intervals
capacity margins
acceptable latency
legal compliance
inventory bands
structural stress limits
error tolerances
oxygen saturation ranges
```

A maintained invariant can be written:

\[
x_\ell(t)\in V_\ell
\quad
\text{for all }t\in H_\ell.
\]

Under uncertainty:

\[
\Pr
\bigl(
x_\ell(t)\in V_\ell
\bigr)
\ge 1-\varepsilon.
\]

Some loci do not preserve a static region.

They preserve a relation, trajectory family, or renewal condition:

```text
complete deliveries within a horizon
restore service after bounded failure
maintain lawful decision procedures
reproduce a cell lineage
preserve semantic compatibility across versions
```

The protected object may therefore be:

```text
a state region
a transition relation
a temporal property
a service envelope
a reconstruction capacity
a future option set
```

## 15. Requirements as Open Realization Obligations

A requirement is not merely absence.

It is an open realization obligation relative to a selected regime.

Represent a requirement as:

\[
r
=
(u,\kappa,\Theta,Q,H,\Gamma,A,G).
\]

Where:

```text
u       = intended consumer
kappa   = capability type
Theta   = contextual conditions
Q       = quality or tolerance envelope
H       = time horizon
Gamma   = acceptance and observation relation
A       = authority or selection source
G       = target or protected relation served
```

The authority term does not imply government authority only.

It may identify:

```text
a design decision
a contract
a physiological necessity
a controller setpoint
a legal obligation
a stakeholder commitment
a learned preference
a scientific acceptance protocol
```

A requirement artifact is distinct from the operational requirement.

For example:

```text
written specification
```

may be interpreted into:

```text
operational acceptance condition.
```

That interpretation is itself a transformation requiring evidence, authority, and context.

---

## 16. Provisions as Exposed Transition Support

A provision is an indexed claim that a locus can support some required transition or state relation.

Represent a provision as:

\[
p
=
(v,\kappa,\iota,\Theta,Q,H,C).
\]

Where:

```text
v       = provider locus
kappa   = capability type
iota    = interface or access point
Theta   = operating conditions
Q       = service envelope
H       = supported horizon
C       = capacity and concurrency conditions
```

A provision is not identical to a resource.

A resource becomes provisionally relevant only when organized into a usable relation.

Thus:

```text
fuel
```

is not automatically:

```text
transport capability.
```

It must participate in a compatible engine, control, vehicle, route, legal, and maintenance structure.

Provision is therefore relationally realized rather than merely possessed.

---

## 17. Capability

A capability is a typed possibility of contributing to a state transition or maintained relation under declared conditions.

A capability type may be represented as:

\[
\kappa:
(S_{\mathrm{in}},\Theta)
\rightharpoonup
S_{\mathrm{out}}.
\]

But the type does not establish that any current provider realizes it.

Distinguish:

```text
capability type
capability claim
currently witnessed capability
maintained capability
invoked capability
realized transition token
```

These correspond roughly to:

```text
what kind of transformation is meant
who claims to supply it
what evidence supports the claim now
whether support persists over time
whether a consumer selected it
what actually happened
```

## 18. Interfaces

An interface is a typed observational and control boundary through which capabilities are requested, exposed, constrained, and evaluated.

An interface may define:

```text
accepted inputs
produced outputs
state assumptions
quality guarantees
failure behavior
timing
capacity
authorization
observability
versioning
```

Represent an interface as:

\[
\iota
=
(K^{-},K^{+},\Theta,Q,\Gamma,F).
\]

Where:

```text
K-minus  = required incoming capability types
K-plus   = exposed outgoing capability types
Theta    = contextual conditions
Q        = quality envelope
Gamma    = observable boundary
F        = declared failure semantics
```

An interface compresses internal organization for a bounded consumer.

The compression is valid only while the hidden realization remains sufficiently maintained.

## 19. Binding

A requirement and a provision do not compose merely because their labels resemble each other.

A binding must establish compatibility.

Let:

\[
\operatorname{Bind}(p,r,c,t)
\]

mean that provision \(p\) satisfies requirement \(r\) in context \(c\) at time \(t\).

Binding may require:

```text
type compatibility
quality sufficiency
capacity sufficiency
timing compatibility
authorization
addressability
reachable access
fresh evidence
acceptable failure behavior
```

A richer binding object is:

\[
b
=
(p,r,\mu,w,c,t),
\]

where:

```text
mu = mediator or bridge path
w  = current witness or evidence
```

Then:

\[
\operatorname{Admissible}(b)
\]

is a decision rather than a naming convention.

## 20. Bridges, Adapters, and Mediators

Direct composition is not always available.

Suppose:

\[
T_1:A\rightharpoonup B
\]

and:

\[
T_2:C\rightharpoonup D.
\]

If \(B\) is not directly compatible with \(C\), a mediator may transform one interface region into another:

\[
M:B\rightharpoonup C.
\]

Then:

\[
T_2\circ M\circ T_1
\]

may become admissible.

Bridges include:

```text
protocol converters
currency exchange
translation
legal recognition
identity federation
mechanical couplers
power converters
customs documentation
metabolic conversion
training
interpretation
```

A bridge is not neutral.

It selects which distinctions are preserved, discarded, translated, rounded, delayed, or made observable.

Every adapter therefore carries policy.

---

## 21. Composition

Composition combines loci or transformations into a larger realization path.

For compatible transformations:

\[
T_1:A\rightharpoonup B,
\qquad
T_2:B\rightharpoonup C,
\]

one may form:

\[
T_2\circ T_1:A\rightharpoonup C.
\]

But practical composition is usually conditional:

\[
T_2\circ_{c,t,w}T_1.
\]

The composition may depend on:

```text
context
time
capacity
witness freshness
shared identity relations
compatible failure semantics
maintained bridges
```

Composition is therefore not mere adjacency.

It is maintained compatibility across a path.

## 22. Realization

A representation names or constrains a possible relation.

A realization is an actual admissible path of state evolution.

For discrete systems:

\[
x_0
\to
x_1
\to
\cdots
\to
x_n.
\]

For continuous systems:

\[
\gamma:[t_0,t_1]\to\mathcal S.
\]

For hybrid systems, a realization may contain:

```text
continuous motion
discrete switching
waiting
message exchange
institutional authorization
human judgment
stochastic events
```

The core requirement is not geometric smoothness.

It is admissible causal and organizational connectivity.

An outcome cannot be counted as realized merely because the initial and final states are named.

## 23. The Core Dynamic Object

The local dynamics of a locus may be written:

\[
x_\ell(t+1)
=
F_\ell
\bigl(
x_\ell(t),
B_\ell(t),
w_\ell(t),
u_\ell(t)
\bigr).
\]

Where:

```text
x_ell(t) = local state
B_ell(t) = currently bound incoming provisions
w_ell(t) = disturbances and exogenous conditions
u_ell(t) = local maintenance or transformation actions
```

The locus also produces:

\[
P_\ell(t)
=
G_\ell
\bigl(x_\ell(t),B_\ell(t),c_t\bigr),
\]

and revises requirements:

\[
R_\ell(t+1)
=
H_\ell
\bigl(
V_\ell,
x_\ell(t+1),
\widehat{x}_\ell,
E_\ell(t)
\bigr).
\]

Thus a node does not merely consume requirements and emit provisions.

It can also generate, reinterpret, prioritize, suspend, or revise requirements.

## 24. Maintenance

Maintenance is the set of transformations that preserve or restore the locus and its relevant relations.

Maintenance may act on:

```text
material structure
software state
interfaces
skills
records
identity relations
provider bindings
legal authority
monitoring systems
recovery capacity
```

Let \(M_\ell\) be a maintenance policy.

A simple viability condition is:

\[
\exists M_\ell
\quad
\forall t\in H_\ell:
\quad
x_\ell(t)\in V_\ell.
\]

A stronger condition includes interface obligations:

\[
\forall t\in H_\ell:
\quad
x_\ell(t)\in V_\ell
\land
\operatorname{BoundaryContract}_\ell(t).
\]

Maintenance is not only repair after failure.

It includes:

```text
inspection
replenishment
training
calibration
replacement
monitoring
renewal
version migration
institutional reproduction
preservation of evidence
```

## 25. Infrastructure

Infrastructure is a persistent network of maintained loci that stabilizes selected classes of realizable paths.

It may be defined as:

\[
\mathcal I_H
=
(
L_H,
B_H,
M_H,
\Sigma_H
),
\]

where:

```text
L_H       = participating loci
B_H       = maintained bindings and bridges
M_H       = maintenance and continuity processes
Sigma_H   = selected target, observer, authority, and horizon regime
```

Infrastructure is therefore not merely a collection of durable objects.

It is maintained relational organization.

A road without repair, legal access, navigation, fuel, vehicles, and control may cease to function as transport infrastructure even if asphalt remains.

A database without current schemas, credentials, storage integrity, software, and operators may cease to function as information infrastructure even if disks remain.

---

## 26. Infrastructure Is Selective

Infrastructure does not preserve all possibilities equally.

It selects and stabilizes some transformations.

A road system favors:

```text
particular origins and destinations
vehicle classes
property arrangements
settlement patterns
commercial flows
jurisdictional priorities
```

A software platform favors:

```text
particular data models
identity assumptions
interaction patterns
hardware architectures
business models
security boundaries
```

A medical system favors particular classifications, access routes, professional roles, treatment models, and evidentiary standards.

Thus:

> Infrastructure is congealed selection expressed as maintained reachability.

Its apparent neutrality often results from the disappearance of historical choices into default interfaces.

## 27. Selection Regimes

To make infrastructure bias explicit, associate each network with a selection regime:

\[
\Sigma
=
(S,G,\Gamma,A,H,J).
\]

Where:

```text
S       = source or admissible initial region
G       = target, viable, or protected region
Gamma   = observation and acceptance relation
A       = authority or selection source
H       = horizon
J       = preference, cost, risk, or priority functional
```

A requirement then means approximately:

> For relevant states in \(S\), preserve or establish an admissible path toward or within \(G\), as judged through \(\Gamma\), under authority \(A\), during horizon \(H\), while considering \(J\).

This connects requirements to their source.

It also reveals that two observers may disagree about whether the same infrastructure succeeds.

## 28. Evidence and Witnesses

A capability claim is not the same as a current capability witness.

Evidence may include:

```text
active probe
inspection
measurement
certificate
transaction history
simulation
formal proof
operator report
legal record
redundant observation
```

Represent evidence as:

\[
e
=
(\varphi,s,m,t,\delta,\rho),
\]

where:

```text
phi     = supported claim
s       = source
m       = method
t       = observation time
delta   = scope and conditions
rho     = confidence or reliability
```

Evidence itself depends on capability networks.

A sensor requires calibration.

A certificate requires authority and identity continuity.

A database record requires storage integrity and interpretation.

There is therefore no automatically privileged evidence layer outside the network.

## 29. Current and Maintained Realizability

For requirement \(r\) and network \(N\), let:

\[
\mathcal P(r,N,c,t)
\]

be the set of admissible provision paths.

Current realizability is:

\[
\operatorname{Realizable}(r,N,c,t)
\iff
\mathcal P(r,N,c,t)\neq\varnothing.
\]

Maintained realizability over horizon \(H\) is:

\[
\operatorname{Maintained}(r,N,H)
\iff
\forall t\in H:
\mathcal P(r,N,t)\neq\varnothing.
\]

Resilient maintained realizability requires independent alternatives rather than merely multiple labels:

\[
\operatorname{Resilient}_k(r,N,H)
\iff
\forall t\in H:
\operatorname{IndependentCapacity}
\bigl(
\mathcal P(r,N,t)
\bigr)
\ge k.
\]

## 30. The Distributed Realization Network

Let:

\[
N=(L,E),
\]

where:

```text
L = maintained loci
E = typed bindings, transformations, authority links, evidence links, and maintenance relations
```

Edges should not all have one meaning.

At minimum distinguish:

```text
requires
provides
binds
mediates
maintains
observes
authorizes
supports as evidence
refines
contains
historically derives from
```

The network is therefore a typed multigraph or hypergraph rather than a simple directed graph.

Some transformations require several jointly available capabilities.

A hyperedge can represent:

\[
\{p_1,p_2,\ldots,p_n\}
\rightharpoonup
q.
\]

This matters because no single upstream provision may be sufficient.

## 31. Cycles and Closure

Realization networks are frequently cyclic.

Examples include:

```text
power systems require control systems
control systems require power
institutions authorize roles
roles reproduce institutions
metabolism maintains membranes
membranes enable metabolism
software maintains records
records guide software recovery
```

A cycle is not automatically invalid.

It may be sustained by:

```text
stored state
external input
initial conditions
periodic replenishment
redundancy
bootstrapping
```

Cyclic realizability may be modeled as a fixed-point or viability problem.

Let \(\Phi_N\) map candidate active provision sets to the provisions they support.

A stable operating closure satisfies:

\[
P^*
\subseteq
\Phi_N(P^*),
\]

or, under a stronger equality convention:

\[
P^*=\Phi_N(P^*).
\]

Maintenance keeps the system near an admissible closure despite disturbance.

---

## 32. Nested Loci

A locus may contain loci.

Let:

\[
\operatorname{Refine}(\ell)
=
N_\ell.
\]

The refined network should preserve the selected external boundary:

\[
\operatorname{Boundary}(N_\ell)
\simeq_{\Gamma,Q,H}
\operatorname{Boundary}(\ell).
\]

This does not require identical internals.

It requires observational equivalence relative to the selected contract.

A heart may be treated as one locus for circulation analysis and decomposed into chambers, valves, conduction tissue, vessels, cells, and molecular processes for another question.

The appropriate depth is decision-relative.

---

## 33. Bounded Agents

A bounded agent has limited:

```text
attention
memory
observation
control bandwidth
energy
time
skill
```

Such an agent cannot reconstruct the entire realization network for every action.

It relies on compressed interfaces:

```text
order
send
compile
drive
approve
activate
```

A small invocation works because maintained loci elsewhere interpret and expand it into a larger realization path.

Thus:

> Boundedness explains the demand for abstraction. Maintained infrastructure explains why abstraction can succeed.

The agent need not be the center of the network.

It is one local locus invoking other loci through selected interfaces.

---

## 34. Local Invocation and Global Dependence

An invocation may be local:

```text
service.compute(x)
```

while realization is distributed across:

```text
serialization
routing
authentication
resource allocation
remote execution
storage
monitoring
power
maintenance
```

The local caller sees one interface.

The global network supplies the hidden middle.

The apparent simplicity of invocation is therefore not evidence of ontological simplicity.

It is evidence of accumulated organization.

---

## 35. What Disappears When Provision Stops?

When provision stops, the provider object may remain materially present.

The first loss is often relational.

A typical degradation sequence is:

```text
1. current executable transition
2. composite capability
3. effective interface
4. trusted evidence
5. maintained distinction
6. agent affordance
7. role or institutional identity
8. future option set
9. reconstruction capacity
10. organization itself
```

For example, when a language community ceases to transmit a technical practice, the tools may remain for a time.

What disappears first may be:

```text
interpretation
repair skill
verification
safe substitution
reproduction of the capability
```

Similarly, when a software service loses maintenance, code may remain while:

```text
dependencies expire
certificates lapse
interfaces drift
operators leave
recovery paths disappear
```

The strongest principle is:

> Provision failure first removes reachable organization; prolonged failure may then remove the organization capable of restoring reachability.

---

## 36. Functional Loss Versus Material Loss

Material persistence and functional persistence are distinct.

A bridge may remain standing but become unusable because:

```text
approach roads fail
inspection lapses
legal access ends
load certification expires
navigation changes
```

A person may retain an anatomical structure while losing a coordinated capability because neural, circulatory, interpretive, or social provision fails.

A server may remain powered while losing service identity because routing, certificates, storage consistency, or authority records fail.

The framework should therefore track:

```text
carrier persistence
organizational persistence
interface persistence
capability persistence
identity persistence
```

separately.

## 37. Reverse Tracing by Reference

A realization network can be investigated backward from an outcome, interface, requirement, or failure.

Begin with a questioned provision:

```text
consumer u
requires capability kappa
```

Then ask:

```text
1. Which interface exposed kappa?
2. Which provider locus occupied that interface?
3. Which implementation realized the provider claim?
4. Which incoming provisions were bound?
5. Which bridges made them compatible?
6. Which maintainers preserved those bindings?
7. Which evidence justified the current claim?
8. Which target or invariant generated the requirement?
9. Which observer defined success?
10. Which authority made the requirement operative?
11. Which historical processes formed the arrangement?
12. Which groups benefit, pay, or remain excluded?
```

This produces several different traces rather than one universal source chain.

---

## 38. Realization Backtrace

A realization backtrace follows operational support:

```text
observed outcome
<-
realized transition
<-
implementation
<-
provider binding
<-
mediators
<-
upstream provisions
<-
maintenance and substrate
```

Formally, for observed claim \(o\), find a subgraph:

\[
N_o\subseteq N
\]

such that:

\[
N_o\Vdash o.
\]

A minimal backtrace may seek a sufficient support set.

A resilience backtrace should also include alternatives, correlated failures, and reconstruction paths.

---

## 39. Requirement Backtrace

A requirement backtrace follows normative or viability support:

```text
binding requirement
<-
operational interpretation
<-
authority or viability relation
<-
evidence and observation
<-
target or protected region
<-
stakeholder, controller, organism, institution, or historical selection process
```

This trace distinguishes:

```text
who wrote the requirement
who interprets it
who authorizes it
who benefits from it
who bears its costs
what failure it prevents
what evidence supports it
```

These roles may be occupied by different loci.

---

## 40. Historical Backtrace

Current operation does not reveal original formation.

A historical backtrace asks:

```text
which earlier problems selected the structure
which actors funded or enforced it
which alternatives were excluded
which accidents became standards
which capabilities were inherited
which present functions were unintended
```

The current target may differ from the original target.

An infrastructure may be repurposed.

A standard may outlive its designers.

A biological structure may be co-opted for a new function.

Therefore distinguish:

```text
current functional dependency
current authority
current beneficiary structure
historical formation
original intention
```

No one trace substitutes for the others.

## 41. Exact Sources and Their Limits

The phrase `the source` is usually ambiguous.

Possible source questions include:

```text
Who currently provides the capability?
Which implementation executes it?
Who maintains the interface?
Who authored the requirement?
Who authorizes it now?
Who produced the evidence?
Which historical process formed the capability?
Which material input carries it?
```

Exact operational source identification is possible when the network preserves:

```text
identity
addressability
provenance
version
binding records
authority records
evidence
time
```

But unique causal source recovery may be impossible because:

```text
causes are joint
records are incomplete
providers are substituted
knowledge becomes tacit
structures emerge incrementally
multiple realization paths converge
```

The appropriate goal is often:

> Recover a sufficient source set for the selected question and horizon.

## 42. Provenance

Provenance records how a claim, state, component, requirement, or capability came to occupy its current role.

Represent provenance as a typed relation:

\[
\operatorname{Prov}(x)
=
\{
(e_i,r_i,t_i,c_i)
\}_{i=1}^{n},
\]

where each entry records:

```text
source entity or event
relation type
time
context
```

Provenance may concern:

```text
material origin
software derivation
institutional authorization
measurement history
requirement interpretation
maintenance history
provider substitution
```

Provenance should not be confused with proof of current usability.

A component may have excellent historical provenance and still be unavailable now.

A current witness and a historical lineage answer different questions.

## 43. Reference-Carrying Interfaces

Reverse tracing becomes practical when interfaces carry references rather than only values.

A provision response may include:

```text

implementation version
observation time
witness source
dependency summary

fallback binding
```

A compact record might resemble:

```yaml

  provider: carrier_B

  context:
    destination: address_17

    contract: order_391

    - customs_clearance

  fallback:
    provider: carrier_C
```

Such records do not expose the entire network.

They preserve enough reopenability for diagnosis and audit.

## 44. Reopenability

Abstraction hides internal detail.

Some hidden detail must remain recoverable.

Reopenability is the ability to selectively expand a locus into the subnetwork relevant to a new question.

A locus should be reopened when:

```text
failure occurs
uncertainty increases
safety is at stake
authority is contested
cost allocation matters
irreversible change is proposed
evidence becomes stale
interfaces drift
```

Reopenability prevents abstraction from becoming uninspectable authority.

## 45. Failure Propagation

A failed provision may propagate through downstream dependency relations.

Let:

\[
\operatorname{Desc}(\ell,\kappa)
\]

be the downstream loci whose admissible paths depend on a provision from \(\ell\).

A simple invalidation rule is:

\[
\operatorname{Invalidate}(e)
=
\operatorname{Descendants}(e).
\]

But propagation is conditional on:

```text
redundancy
stored state
capacity margins
fallback providers
time buffers
substitution cost
correlated failure
```

A more realistic failure update computes which paths remain admissible after removing or degrading an edge.

## 46. Viability Margins

A locus may currently operate while remaining close to failure.

Define a margin:

\[
m_\ell(x,c)
\]

as the distance, slack, or probability-adjusted reserve between the current state and a selected failure boundary.

Margins may concern:

```text
energy
inventory
time
latency
capital
staffing
structural load
attention
legal authority
trust
```

Current success does not establish robust maintained realizability.

A system may have one valid path with negligible margin.

The framework should therefore evaluate:

```text
path existence
path capacity
path independence
recovery time
reconstruction cost
```

## 47. Generative Centrality

Some loci matter because they currently carry high flow.

Others matter because they preserve the ability to generate many future paths.

Define generative centrality informally as the effect of a locus on the future reachable capability set.

For locus \(\ell\):

\[
GC(\ell)
=
\Delta
\left|
\mathcal R_{\mathrm{future}}
\right|
\]

under removal, degradation, or preservation of \(\ell\).

Examples include:

```text
repair skills
source code
seed stock
standards
training systems
backup identity records
manufacturing tools
scientific methods
```

These may be rarely invoked during nominal operation yet remain central to reconstruction.

## 59. The Central Building Block

The minimal building block is not:

```text
resource
```

and not merely:

```text
requirement -> provision.
```

It is:

> A situated locus that distinguishes some state relations as operative, binds incoming capabilities, transforms local organization, exposes outgoing capabilities, observes results, and participates in the maintenance or revision of those distinctions over time.

This building block can be conscious or non-conscious, centralized or distributed, biological or technical, physical or institutional.

Its unity is relational rather than material.

## 60. Tests for Identifying a Locus

A candidate system is usefully modeled as a maintained locus when most of the following questions have meaningful answers:

```text
1. What gives it identity across change?
2. How can it be addressed or distinguished?
3. What boundary is being selected?
4. What state does it retain?
5. What target, invariant, or service relation does it preserve?
6. Which incoming capabilities does it require?
7. Which outgoing capabilities does it expose?
8. Through which interfaces are those relations mediated?
9. What transformations occur locally?
10. What evidence indicates current realizability?
11. What processes maintain or restore it?
12. What disappears when its provisions fail?
13. How can it be refined into a subnetwork?
14. How can its authority and provenance be traced?
```

If none of these can be specified, `locus` may be too strong or the analytical boundary may be poorly chosen.

## 61. Design Implications

### Make target selection explicit

Do not model requirements without recording the target, authority, observer, and horizon that make them operative.

### Separate identity from state

Allow components, providers, and implementations to change without silently changing the identity relation being preserved.

### Separate addressability from accessibility

A named provider is not necessarily reachable, authorized, compatible, or sufficient.

### Carry provenance and witnesses

Claims about provision should include enough evidence and lineage to support current decisions and later diagnosis.

### Preserve reopenability

Interfaces should hide unnecessary detail without making critical dependencies permanently uninspectable.

### Model maintenance as first-class

Do not treat continuity as automatic after initial construction.

### Track failure propagation and reconstruction

Removal analysis should include lost future capability and recovery cost, not only present throughput.

### Represent infrastructure as selective

Record whose targets, access patterns, and constraints the infrastructure stabilizes.

---

## 62. Failure Modes of the Ontology

### Reifying the locus

A locus is an analytical and operational unit, not necessarily a naturally bounded object.

### Treating every pattern as equivalent

Shared relational structure does not erase material, legal, biological, or ethical differences.

### Hiding authority inside requirements

A requirement label should not conceal who made it binding or whose interests it serves.

### Confusing current success with maintained viability

One successful invocation does not establish continuity.

### Confusing identity with uninterrupted material persistence

Functional, legal, historical, and material identities may diverge.

### Assuming unique causal sources

Distributed systems often have joint, substituted, or historically layered sources.

### Assuming local optimization preserves global viability

A locus may improve its own margin while degrading the larger network.

### Treating evidence as external and infallible

Evidence is itself produced by maintained capability networks.

## 63. Research Questions

### Identity

Which identity relations should be preserved under replacement, refinement, migration, and repair?

### Boundary choice

How can a boundary be selected without hiding decision-relevant costs, dependencies, or affected populations?

### Authority

How should conflicting authorities and target regimes be represented in one network?

### Viability

### Composition

Which compatibility laws are required for reliable composition across heterogeneous domains?

### Evidence

How should witness freshness, scope, confidence, and dependency be propagated through recursive provision networks?

### Infrastructure bias

How can the selected source and target populations of an infrastructure be inferred from its interfaces, capacities, exclusions, and maintenance patterns?

### Backtracking

What is the minimal reference structure required to recover sufficient realization and requirement traces after failure?

### Historical formation

How should current function be separated from original intention, path dependence, and later repurposing?

### Failure

Which relational structures disappear before material destruction becomes visible?

### Reconstruction

How should generative centrality and reconstruction cost affect maintenance and removal decisions?

### Distribution

When should a network be treated as one macro-locus, and when should it remain decomposed?

### Ethics and governance

How can affected but non-authorizing populations be represented in target selection and observational boundaries?

### Mathematics

Can maintained loci be formalized through a compositional theory that integrates typed interfaces, dynamic state, viability, evidence, and authority without collapsing their differences?

## 64. Central Principles

### Locus Principle

> The reusable unit of analysis is a maintained locus of constrained realization rather than an isolated object, resource, capability, or requirement.

### Distributed-Organization Principle

> What appears as one organization at one scale may be a recursively coupled network of loci at another; no decomposition level is automatically privileged.

### Selection-Regime Principle

> Requirements become operative only relative to a target region, observer, authority or viability source, context, and horizon.

### Relational-Capability Principle

> A capability is a typed transition possibility exposed by a locus under conditions, not a context-free substance possessed by an object.

### Requirement-Polarity Principle

> A requirement is an open incoming capability obligation relative to a selected locus and target regime.

### Provision-Polarity Principle

> A provision is an outgoing capability claim that becomes effective only through an admissible binding relation.

### Addressability Principle

> Existence, identity, addressability, accessibility, authorization, compatibility, and sufficiency are distinct conditions.

### Interface Principle

> An interface compresses internal realization while declaring the boundary conditions under which a locus can participate in composition.

### Bridge-Policy Principle

> Every bridge or adapter preserves some distinctions and transforms or discards others; mediation therefore carries policy.

### Realization Principle

> A represented relation succeeds only through an admissible path of actual state and organizational transition.

### Maintenance Principle

> Continued capability depends on preserving providers, interfaces, evidence, bindings, recovery relations, and the organization that reproduces them.

### Infrastructure-Selectivity Principle

> Infrastructure stabilizes selected regions of reachability and therefore embodies historical priorities, exclusions, and target populations.

### Failure-Loss Principle

> Provision failure may first destroy accessibility, composability, and reproducibility before destroying material carriers.

### Reopenability Principle

> Abstraction remains trustworthy only when hidden structure can be selectively reopened for diagnosis, safety, authority, and revision.

### Provenance Principle

> Current usability and historical origin are distinct; both require explicit references when they matter.

### Bounded-Agent Principle

> Bounded agents invoke local interfaces because maintained distributed organization performs the omitted realization elsewhere.

### No-Privileged-Meta-Level Principle

> Requirement production, evaluation, authority, evidence, and modeling are themselves realized through capability networks.

## 65. Central Statements

> Objects do not require or provide in isolation; loci participate in indexed requirement and provision relations.

> The whole organization is one possible view, not the universal primitive.

> A maintained locus may be spatially distributed, institutionally distributed, temporally distributed, or recursively nested.

> Identity tells us what is being followed across change; addressability tells us how it can be located; accessibility tells us whether a selected consumer can currently reach it.

> A requirement is not merely a missing resource. It is an open realization obligation under a selection regime.

> A provision is not merely possessed material. It is an exposed, admissible transition-supporting relation.

> Interfaces are boundaries of selective observability and control.

> Bridges do not erase difference. They organize compatibility.

> Infrastructure is maintained reachability for selected agents, targets, and conditions.

> When provision stops, reachable organization may disappear before matter does.

> Reverse tracing should follow realization, requirement, authority, evidence, maintenance, and historical provenance as distinct graphs.

> The deepest persistent object is not one provider but a recursively maintained relation among loci.

> Civilization, organisms, software systems, and institutions differ materially while sharing patterns of distributed maintenance.

## 66. Conclusion

A framework of capabilities, interfaces, realization, composition, infrastructure, and bounded agents can appear disconnected because these concepts do not identify the system that makes them operative.

Capabilities do not float independently.

Requirements do not arise from objects alone.

Interfaces do not select their own observers.

Infrastructure does not choose its own beneficiaries.

Realization paths do not maintain themselves.

The missing center is a distributed organization of loci that preserve selected regions of realizability through time.

A maintained locus has:

```text
identity
boundary
state
retained organization
target or viability regime
incoming requirements
outgoing provisions
interfaces
transformation policies
maintenance
observation
evidence
provenance
```

No locus is necessarily self-sufficient.

Each may depend on upstream loci and provide capabilities downstream.

Large organizations emerge as recursive compositions of such loci.

At one scale a hospital, organism, software service, transport network, or institution may be modeled as one locus.

At another scale each decomposes into a distributed network of maintained loci.

The ontology is therefore neither purely object-centered nor purely process-centered.

It is relational, dynamic, distributed, and maintenance-centered.

Its central object is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{a maintained locus of constrained realization}
}
\]

Its central network is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{a recursively coupled field of such loci}
}
\]

Its central question is not merely:

```text
What exists?
```

It is:

```text
Which organization preserves which realizable relations,
for whom or for what target,
through which interfaces and dependencies,
under whose authority or viability conditions,
with what evidence,
and for how long?
```

This question connects ontology to engineering.

It connects engineering to logistics, anatomy, computation, institutions, and ecology.

It explains why local abstractions can be effective without becoming magical.

It explains why infrastructures are powerful without being neutral.

It explains why a provider can disappear while a capability survives, and why material objects can remain while an organization loses its function.

It also supplies a method of inquiry:

```text
identify the locus
state the selection regime
trace requirements
bind provisions
inspect interfaces
follow realization paths
measure margins
preserve evidence
reopen hidden dependencies
backtrack authority and provenance
observe what fails when provision stops
```

The resulting framework is not a theory of one whole organization.

It is a theory of how distributed organizations become locally invocable, recursively maintainable, selectively observable, and historically traceable.
