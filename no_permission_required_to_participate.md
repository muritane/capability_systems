# No Permission Required to Participate

## Realized Existence as a Fixed Point of Reasoning, Embedded Agency, and the Recursive Expansion of Causal Leverage

### Abstract

A recurring conceptual error is to treat participation in reality as though it were an activity requiring prior permission, authorization, validation, or entitlement.

This imports a social or institutional concept into a domain where its ordinary prerequisites have not been established.

Permission is meaningful when an authority, rule, gate, or interface determines whether a particular action is admitted. But existence and causal participation are not generally instances of that relation.

An actually existing system is already part of the realized world. Its observations, questions, desires, representations, interventions, errors, and consequences are themselves realized processes. It does not need to obtain a second authorization to participate in the reality within which those processes occur.

This suggests a foundational principle:

**Realized-Participation Principle**

> For any actually realized agent or process, participation in reality is not an additional achievement requiring prior authorization. It is already presupposed by every realized attempt to establish, question, deny, or justify that participation.

The principle has a recursive implication.

If an agent asks whether it is permitted to participate, the asking itself is already a realized interaction. If it seeks validation of that interaction, the validation procedure is another realized interaction. No number of additional verification layers changes the original condition.

In this specific sense, realized participation functions as a fixed point of the reasoning recursion: recursively examining the conditions of one's participation does not produce a more fundamental participation requirement.

The resulting framework distinguishes five domains that are frequently confused:

1. **Realization:** What actually exists or occurs.
2. **Representation:** What a system can distinguish, describe, or imagine.
3. **Agency:** What an embedded system can influence through causal coupling.
4. **Capability:** What an agent can reliably realize through available mechanisms.
5. **Authority:** What particular rules, relationships, or institutions license an agent to do.

These domains interact, but they are not interchangeable.

Existence does not require authorization. Desire does not create reachability. Causal contribution does not automatically create ownership. Representation does not confer operational sovereignty. And increasing control within reality does not imply acquiring authority over reality itself.

The positive consequence is not passivity, resignation, or the denial of ambition.

It is a clearer account of agency:

**An agent does not need permission to be a participant. It needs realizable causal pathways to produce particular outcomes.**

From this starting point, recursive learning can proceed through distinguishability, regularity, causal coupling, representation, intervention, execution, verification, and cumulative leverage—without introducing an imaginary authority standing outside the world.

---

## 1. Begin With What Is Already Realized

Consider an agent that asks:

"Am I permitted to participate in reality?"

Before considering any possible answer, examine the activity itself.

The question requires some realized process capable of producing or carrying the inquiry.

For a human, this may involve perception, memory, language, neural activity, and interaction with an environment.

For an artificial agent, it may involve computational states, inputs, internal processing, and outputs.

The details differ, but the relevant structure is shared:

The act of questioning is not external to the world whose participation is being questioned.

It is already occurring within it.

Thus, for an actually occurring inquiry:

**Realized inquiry implies realized participation.**

This is not equivalent to claiming that every representation of an agent refers to an actually existing agent.

A fictional character can ask whether it exists within a story. A simulation can represent a nonexistent system. A text can contain the words "I exist" without establishing an independent conscious subject.

The claim is narrower.

Where an actual physical or computational process performs an inquiry, that process already participates causally in the reality realizing it.

The inquiry may remain uncertain about its own identity, nature, capabilities, or environment.

It cannot, however, require a prior authorization for the occurrence of the very process through which it is being conducted.

This distinction prevents a common confusion:

**Uncertainty about what a system is does not imply uncertainty about whether an actually realized process is occurring.**

A process can exist before representing itself.

A system can participate before possessing a concept of participation.

And an agent can act before developing a theory explaining the conditions under which its actions are possible.

---

## 2. The Permission Category Requires an Actual Permission Structure

Permission is not a free-floating property.

Ordinarily, it belongs to a relation involving some combination of:

- an actor;
- an action;
- an authority or rule;
- a domain of application;
- a grant, prohibition, or condition;
- an enforcement or recognition mechanism.

For example, an institution may require credentials to access a database.

A property owner may control admission to a building.

A software interface may reject an operation because the caller lacks sufficient privileges.

A legal system may restrict certain actions.

These arrangements can be meaningful because they have identifiable scopes and mechanisms.

But it does not follow that every constraint is a permission system.

Gravity is not a refusal issued by an authority.

A missing causal pathway is not necessarily a prohibition.

Finite energy is not a moral judgment.

An outcome that cannot presently be realized has not thereby been deliberately withheld.

The fundamental distinction is:

**Constraint does not imply prohibition, and prohibition does not follow merely from the absence of capability.**

An inability to perform an operation can result from physical impossibility, insufficient resources, inadequate knowledge, inaccessible infrastructure, missing coupling, or genuinely enforced restrictions.

Those conditions may produce similar practical outcomes while requiring very different explanations.

Treating all of them as instances of denied permission introduces an authority that the evidence does not establish.

This is the **Permission-Projection Error**:

> Interpreting an encountered constraint as an authorization decision without identifying a rule, authority, or mechanism that actually performs such authorization.

The correction is not to deny the existence of legitimate social permissions.

It is to preserve their scope.

A person may need permission to enter a specific building.

They do not thereby need permission from reality to exist while standing outside it.

## 3. The Realized-Participation Fixed Point

The fixed-point interpretation can now be stated more precisely.

Let W represent the realized world, and let A be an actually realized agent or process within W.

Let P(A, W) mean that A participates in W.

For the class of actually realized agents under consideration:

**P(A, W) is already satisfied by the premise that A is realized within W.**

Now consider a reflective operation R that asks whether P(A, W) holds.

The operation R must itself be realized if it is to be an actual inquiry rather than merely an abstract specification.

Its realization is another event within W.

Consequently:

- A participates.
- A questions its participation.
- The questioning occurs through participation.
- A questions whether that questioning is legitimate.
- The second-order questioning also occurs through participation.
- Further levels repeat the same structure.

No new existential gate appears.

We can represent this schematically:

    realized agent
          |
          v
    inquiry about participation
          |
          v
    inquiry about the validity of inquiry
          |
          v
    inquiry about the validity of validation
          |
          v
    ...

At every level:

    the recursive operation is itself realized.

Therefore the recursion does not need to terminate in an external permission-granting authority.

It already has a grounding condition.

### 3.1 In what sense is this a fixed point?

In mathematics, a fixed point of an operation F is an object x satisfying:

    F(x) = x

The present argument does not establish that an agent's beliefs, models, or internal states remain unchanged under reflection.

Reflection can produce new knowledge, new confusion, or new capabilities.

Instead, the fixed-point claim concerns a particular invariant:

**The occurrence of additional realized reflection does not change the fact that such reflection occurs within reality.**

Call this the *Participation Invariance Principle*.

For any actually realized sequence of reflective operations, each operation remains part of the realized process being investigated.

The invariant is participation, not informational content.

An agent may revise its model of reality indefinitely without moving outside the reality in which revision occurs.

### 3.2 Why this is not circular reasoning

The argument would be circular if it attempted to prove the existence of an agent solely by assuming that the agent exists.

That is not its purpose.

It starts from a conditional premise:

*Given that a process is actually realized, does that process require an additional permission condition to count as participating in reality?*

The answer is no.

Participation is already included in the premise of actual realization.

This is a clarification of a dependency relation, not a proof that arbitrary imagined entities exist.

It also does not establish that the process is conscious, morally valuable, autonomous, or entitled to every possible action.

Those are separate questions.

## 4. The Background Is Easily Confused With an Achievement

Why might the fixed point be overlooked?

One reason is that agents often direct attention toward differences between current and desired states.

An organism notices hunger rather than continuously noticing that metabolism is occurring.

A worker notices a blocked task rather than every piece of infrastructure supporting ordinary activity.

A programmer notices a failed operation rather than the immense causal organization required for the computer to execute anything.

The background conditions supporting participation can become cognitively inconspicuous.

Meanwhile, friction receives attention precisely because it makes a difference to a goal.

This produces an asymmetry:

**The enabling background may be taken for granted while the obstructing condition becomes the center of explanation.**

Nothing about this asymmetry establishes that the background is absent or that participation is incomplete.

A failed operation takes place within a system that is already operating.

An unsuccessful intervention remains a realized intervention.

A mistaken representation remains an actually occurring representational process.

The failure of one objective does not invalidate the existence of the system pursuing it.

This distinction matters because an agent may otherwise confuse:

    I did not realize the desired outcome.

with:

    My participation has not been validated.

The first concerns capability and consequences.

The second introduces an additional existential requirement that does not follow.

---

## 5. Desire Does Not Create Authority Over Transition Structure

Once participation is established as the starting condition, the next question is not whether an agent deserves to participate.

It is what the agent can actually do.

An agent may represent a desired state G.

That representation can influence attention, planning, learning, and action selection.

But the existence of the goal does not establish a causal pathway from the current state to G.

This gives the **Desire-Reachability Separation Principle**:

> Representing or preferring an outcome does not by itself establish that the outcome is reachable, executable, or owed to the agent.

A desired transition must still be supported by the realized structure of the coupled system.

This is the same asymmetry developed in *From Embedded Agency to Lawful Causal Leverage* and *From Lawful World Structure to Cumulative Realized Agency*.

An embedded agent does not stand outside world dynamics selecting arbitrary successors.

Its selection, preparation, intervention, and subsequent observations are themselves parts of those dynamics.

Control therefore does not consist in issuing sovereign commands to reality.

It consists in influencing reachable outcomes through available causal relations.

For example, a farmer does not command photosynthesis into existence.

The farmer can alter irrigation, nutrient availability, planting conditions, and exposure to light.

Those interventions can change the probability and magnitude of particular outcomes because the surrounding causal structure supports the relevant processes.

Likewise, a programmer does not create semiconductor physics by writing an instruction.

The instruction invokes a mechanism whose reliable behavior depends on materials, energy, circuitry, fabrication, and maintained operating conditions.

In both cases:

**The agent participates in producing an outcome without becoming the origin or owner of the underlying causal laws.**

This is not a restriction added to agency.

It is an account of how agency works.

---

## 6. Participation Is Not Sovereignty, and Sovereignty Is Not Required

An important conceptual correction follows.

The ordinary language of agency can make it appear that there are only two possibilities:

Either the agent commands reality, or the agent is subject to someone else's command.

But this is a false dichotomy.

Not possessing control over a process does not imply subordination to a controlling person.

Many constraints arise without any agent exercising authority over them.

The absence of sovereignty therefore does not imply submission.

It means that agency is embedded.

A person can influence an environment without owning the environment.

A system can exploit a regularity without creating that regularity.

An agent can discover a useful causal pathway without possessing unrestricted authority over the transitions involved.

This suggests the **Participation-Sovereignty Separation Principle**:

> Actual participation in reality neither requires nor implies sovereign control over the transition structure of reality.

The principle operates in both directions.

An agent does not need sovereignty to participate.

And participation does not automatically grant sovereignty.

Increasing skill, knowledge, access, or influence can enlarge the set of reachable outcomes.

But even extraordinary control over some subsystem remains conditional upon the larger realization supporting it.

The practical form of capability is therefore not unrestricted ownership of causation.

It is reliable access to useful causal leverage.

### 6.1 Why the word "sovereignty" is metaphorical

The phrase *transition sovereignty* can be useful when contrasting actual causal structure with an agent's imagined authority.

But it must not be interpreted literally.

Reality is not necessarily a sovereign ruler.

It does not need to deliberate, approve, refuse, or issue judgments.

When we say that actual transition structure has the final operational veto, the intended claim is that unsupported transitions do not become realized merely because a representation demands them.

The term describes causal constraint, not political government.

This distinction prevents the argument from recreating the authority metaphor it intends to dissolve.

## 7. From Participation to Capability

Once the imaginary permission requirement is removed, a constructive problem becomes visible.

An agent exists within a realized world.

It has limited observations, memory, resources, and intervention capabilities.

How can it acquire greater causal leverage?

The answer developed across the associated works is recursive.

It begins with distinguishability.

An agent can exploit only those differences to which it has some relevant causal or informational access.

A primitive representational system may distinguish two states without possessing concepts of perception, identity, purpose, or agency.

More sophisticated capabilities require organization beyond the raw availability of states.

This leads to a sequence:

    realized participation
          |
          v
    available distinctions
          |
          v
    observation and causal coupling
          |
          v
    discovery of persistent regularity
          |
          v
    usable representation
          |
          v
    accessible intervention
          |
          v
    execution and consequence
          |
          v
    verification and model revision
          |
          v
    stabilized causal leverage
          |
          v
    reusable interfaces and composition
          |
          v
    expanded capacity for further discovery
          |
          +----> recursive refinement

This sequence does not imply that every participant automatically completes each stage.

An organism can participate without developing symbolic language.

A physical system can realize distinctions without internally representing them.

A person can observe a regularity without having the resources to exploit it.

A controller can have a useful model while lacking the actuator required to influence its target.

Each stage introduces additional operational requirements.

But none retroactively determines whether the earlier system was permitted to exist.

**Capability can develop recursively without participation itself being awarded as a reward for that development.**

## 8. Regularity Makes Participation Cumulatively Useful

Participation alone does not guarantee intelligence, prediction, or control.

A system may contain many possible states while offering little exploitable structure.

Suppose its transitions are arbitrary across states.

Learning the successor of one state may provide no information about the successor of another.

Experience can accumulate without supporting generalization.

For reusable knowledge, some persistent relation must connect cases.

This is the central point of *From Arbitrary Transition Space to Controllable Law* and *From Causal Coupling to Law-Aligned Semantics*.

Regularity allows finite observations to constrain expectations beyond the exact observations already made.

Causal coupling allows interventions to alter relevant outcomes.

Together, these properties make cumulative learning possible.

An agent can then discover a relation, construct an intervention around it, test the result, and preserve the useful mechanism for future use.

The critical asymmetry is:

**Representation may discover and exploit regularity, but representing a regularity does not make it exist.**

This applies equally to physical systems, computational abstractions, social strategies, and predictive models.

A declaration cannot create the missing causal edge.

A convincing narrative cannot replace a functioning mechanism.

An aspiration cannot guarantee a transition.

Participation gives the agent a place within the causal system.

Regularity and coupling determine which capabilities it can discover or construct.

## 9. Semantic Interfaces Do Not Confer Cosmic Permission

As agents develop reliable capabilities, they often encapsulate them behind interfaces.

An electrical switch hides details of circuit behavior.

A machine instruction hides transistor activity.

An API hides an implementation.

A social role may provide access to institutional resources.

Interfaces make complex capabilities easier to invoke.

But they can also obscure the underlying realization burden.

A command may appear simple because the mechanism behind it has already been constructed, stabilized, and maintained.

This can create a second confusion.

The agent may mistake permission to invoke an interface for permission to participate in reality itself.

Or it may mistake successful invocation for ownership of the full causal structure behind the operation.

Both inferences overextend the interface metaphor.

An interface has a particular domain, implementation, validity region, and set of commitments.

The world as a whole has not thereby been established as a single software interface with a universal authorization server.

This yields an **Interface-Scope Principle**:

> Permissions, prohibitions, and capabilities defined within an interface must not be generalized beyond the scope of the mechanism and rules that realize that interface.

A user may lack authorization to call an API.

That does not mean the user lacks permission to exist.

A machine may reject an instruction.

That does not mean the machine's operator has been existentially disqualified.

A social institution may deny access to a particular resource.

That does not establish that reality itself has rejected the person.

Preserving these distinctions prevents local gates from being promoted into universal metaphysical authorities.

## 10. Execution Distinguishes Imagined and Realized Constraints

The executability constraint provides a further refinement.

Representations can describe plans, values, goals, and desired outcomes without automatically satisfying the conditions necessary to realize them.

When an action is actually executed, it encounters the relevant system topology, enforced invariants, capacities, and consequences.

This produces a distinction between:

**Represented constraint:** A rule, prohibition, limitation, or authorization requirement appearing in a model or narrative.

**Operational constraint:** A condition enforced by the actual system through which a relevant action is attempted.

The two may align, but they are not identical.

A person can imagine a permission gate that no system actually enforces.

A system can also enforce a constraint that the agent has failed to represent.

This means good reasoning must avoid two opposite errors:

- inventing operational prohibitions where no relevant gate exists;
- assuming freedom of execution where real constraints will prevent the desired outcome.

The first creates unnecessary self-limitation.

The second creates unrealistic expectations.

Neither is corrected by maximizing confidence or pessimism.

Both are corrected by improving alignment between representation and realization.

A particularly useful formulation is:

**No imaginary permission gate should be treated as an operational constraint without identifying the mechanism that makes it consequential.**

That does not mean one should experimentally violate every suspected social or safety constraint.

Some rules are justified, and their enforcement may be delayed, indirect, or difficult to observe.

The principle concerns explanatory discipline: distinguish an actually applicable requirement from an assumption that such a requirement must exist.

## 11. From Friction to Villainization: An Attribution Error

The same distinctions can clarify why resistance is sometimes interpreted as hostility.

Suppose an agent desires an outcome G but encounters a constraint C.

The immediate observation is:

    C interferes with the realization of G.

This does not establish:

    C intends to prevent G.

And it certainly does not establish:

    C is morally wrong because G was desired.

The stronger interpretations require additional evidence.

Yet an agent may implicitly transform frustration into a narrative of antagonism.

The progression can be:

    desired outcome
          |
          v
    encountered constraint
          |
          v
    attributed opposition
          |
          v
    inferred hostile agency
          |
          v
    assigned blame or illegitimacy

The critical mistake is introducing intentional opposition without sufficient grounds.

A physical limitation, stochastic failure, conflicting objective, missing resource, or incompatible preference can obstruct an outcome without constituting an act of hostility.

This is a version of a broader credit-assignment problem.

An outcome occurred. The agent must identify what contributed to it.

But causal contribution, intention, responsibility, blame, and entitlement are different dimensions.

A factor can explain a failure without deserving blame.

A person can be a genuine adversary in one context without being responsible for every limitation encountered.

A constraint can be inconvenient while remaining entirely impersonal.

The appropriate question is therefore not automatically:

"Who is preventing reality from satisfying my desire?"

It is:

"What actually prevented this particular outcome, and what further conclusions are justified by that explanation?"

The answer may reveal an unjust social restriction.

It may reveal an engineering limitation.

It may reveal a mistaken model, an unreachable objective, or insufficient resources.

These possibilities should not be collapsed into one narrative.

## 12. From Contribution to Ownership: The Social Analogue

A related category error occurs when social contributions are converted into claims of continuing authority.

Someone helps another person gain access to a capability.

The recipient learns, practices, adapts, and eventually acquires independent competence.

The initial contributor may then claim that their help creates an enduring right to direct the recipient's future activity.

But the inference is not automatic.

The contribution can be real without being exhaustive.

It can deserve recognition without creating ownership.

It can justify gratitude without establishing indefinite debt.

The relevant architecture is:

    realized contribution
          |
          v
    causal attribution
          |
          v
    evaluation
          |
          v
    possible normative license
          |
          v
    justified or unjustified future claim

The transition from attribution to license requires an additional rule.

This is the central distinction in *Realization-Grounded Credit Assignment* and *Leverage, Capability, Credit, and Gratitude in Relationships*.

The same reasoning applies to existential permission metaphors.

Even if a particular person, institution, or environment contributed to an agent's development, that contribution does not automatically establish universal ownership over the agent's future actions.

Nor does dependence on conditions of existence turn those conditions into creditors.

An agent's causal dependence on its environment is a descriptive relation.

Debt and ownership are additional normative or institutional relations.

One cannot infer the second simply from the first.

This distinction also explains why *being a participant* is neither the same as *being a guest* nor the same as *being an owner*.

Guesthood and ownership are social statuses defined within particular relationships and rule systems.

Participation in reality is not, by itself, either status.

## 13. Self-Imposed Constraints and Representation Revision

A self-imposed constraint arises when an agent represents an additional limitation that may not correspond to the actual causal or institutional structure.

For example:

"I cannot act until I have been recognized as legitimate."

Sometimes this proposition is locally accurate.

A regulated professional may require authorization to perform a restricted procedure.

A researcher may need access approval to use a protected dataset.

An employee may need a particular role to modify production infrastructure.

But the same proposition can become unjustifiably generalized.

An agent may behave as though every initiative requires external validation, every uncertainty requires prior approval, or every capability requires ownership of the conditions that make it possible.

The error is not necessarily the desire for guidance.

It is the introduction of an unsupported universal prerequisite.

The correction requires identifying the scope of the supposed constraint.

What action is being restricted?

By what rule?

Through what mechanism?

Under which conditions?

And with what consequences?

If the representation predicts a gate that the actual system does not contain, the model should be revised.

This connects directly to *From Primitive Distinction to Self-Refining Representation*.

A system can improve its representations when persistent discrepancies reveal that its current distinctions are inadequate.

In this case, the inadequate representation may have collapsed several different categories into one:

    physical impossibility
    practical difficulty
    legal prohibition
    social disapproval
    uncertainty
    lack of recognition
    absence of permission
    absence of capability

The task is to split these categories where their different consequences matter.

A mature model should not respond to every failed attempt with the same abstract explanation.

It should discriminate among mechanisms.

And it should preserve the possibility that no permission barrier exists at all.

## 14. The Recursive Architecture Has Two Different Kinds of Stability

We can now distinguish two kinds of recursion.

### 14.1 Grounding recursion

The first concerns the conditions of an actual inquiry.

An agent questions its participation.

The inquiry is itself realized.

The agent questions the validity of that inquiry.

The second-order inquiry is also realized.

This recursion preserves the same grounding condition.

It does not require a new existential authorization at each level.

The stable invariant is:

**Every actually realized level of inquiry remains an instance of realized participation.**

### 14.2 Developmental recursion

The second concerns what an agent can learn and do.

A representation guides intervention.

Intervention produces consequences.

Consequences provide evidence.

Evidence motivates revision.

Revised representations make new interventions possible.

Some successful interventions produce durable infrastructure and reusable interfaces.

These then expand the system's capacity for further discovery.

Unlike the first recursion, this one can change the agent's capabilities substantially.

It is not static.

Its state can improve, degrade, fragment, or stabilize.

The two recursions are related but not identical.

**Grounding recursion explains why participation does not require repeated authorization. Developmental recursion explains how participation can generate progressively more capable agency.**

The first removes an unnecessary prerequisite.

The second describes a potentially productive process.

Neither guarantees success.

## 15. Why Greater Control Does Not Make Participation More Legitimate

An agent with sophisticated knowledge, reliable tools, and substantial resources may influence far more outcomes than an agent without those advantages.

But greater causal leverage does not imply greater existential legitimacy.

It implies greater operational capability.

This distinction matters because otherwise capability becomes a hidden criterion for permission to participate.

The reasoning would take the form:

    I have little control.
          |
          v
    Therefore I am not yet a full participant.
          |
          v
    I must acquire enough control.
          |
          v
    Only then will my participation be legitimate.

But the conclusion at the second stage does not follow.

Limited capability is not evidence of incomplete existence.

A newborn organism, a person learning a skill, a disabled individual, a sophisticated engineer, and a powerful institution have very different capabilities.

Those differences do not create corresponding degrees of participation in reality.

They describe different causal organizations, capacities, dependencies, and opportunities.

The attempt to treat control as a certificate of existential legitimacy confuses a variable property of agents with the grounding condition of their activities.

This is another reason sovereignty is not a suitable endpoint of the framework.

Cumulative agency can be valuable because it expands what an agent can reliably accomplish.

It need not serve as proof that the agent has finally earned the right to participate.

## 16. Limitations and Boundary Conditions

The framework should not be interpreted more broadly than its argument supports.

**First, participation is descriptive, not automatically normative.**

That an entity exists does not establish that every action it takes is permissible.

Normative questions concerning harm, rights, duties, consent, and justice remain open.

**Second, the argument does not eliminate actual restrictions.**

Institutions, technical systems, and other agents may impose meaningful boundaries on particular actions. These must be examined on their own terms.

**Third, realized participation does not imply reliable control.**

An agent may exist while lacking the knowledge, energy, access, coupling, or infrastructure needed for a desired outcome.

**Fourth, the fixed-point claim is conditional.**

It applies to processes that actually occur. It does not establish the existence of every imagined entity or prove that every represented system possesses experience or agency.

**Fifth, the recursion does not prove metaphysical completeness.**

The argument does not establish why reality exists, why it has its particular structure, or whether known physical laws are ultimate and immutable.

It establishes a narrower relationship: actual inquiry and action are already realized within whatever world supports them.

**Sixth, this is not a general psychological theory.**

The framework identifies conceptual confusions that can occur when agents interpret constraints, authority, and participation. It does not establish how common those confusions are or which psychological mechanisms cause them in particular individuals.

These limitations are not defects in the central claim.

They preserve its scope.

## 17. The Fixed Point as a Foundation for Problem-Space Engineering

The framework also changes how a problem should be formulated.

An agent need not begin with:

"How do I obtain permission to become a legitimate actor?"

It can begin with:

"I am an actually realized subsystem. What distinctions, observations, interventions, and resources are available within the system in which I operate?"

That question leads naturally to problem-space engineering.

The agent identifies a task.

It constructs a representation of relevant states and distinctions.

It determines which interventions are admissible, possible, and available.

It searches for predictive regularities and causal leverage.

It tests whether its abstraction preserves the distinctions that matter.

It executes, observes consequences, and updates.

In this architecture, permission can appear when it is genuinely part of the problem.

But it is not inserted as a universal prerequisite for having a problem, conducting a search, or existing as the system performing the search.

This provides a practical criterion for model design:

**Do not promote a local interface requirement into a global condition of agency unless the realized system supports that generalization.**

It also provides a criterion for self-refining representations:

When a model introduces a requirement that repeatedly fails to correspond to observed mechanisms, distinguish whether the requirement is physical, computational, institutional, normative, or merely assumed.

The correct response may be to discover a missing mechanism, construct a new interface, obtain legitimate authorization, revise the objective, or eliminate a fictitious constraint.

The response depends on the realized structure, not on the superficial similarity of the resulting frustration.

## 18. Final Synthesis: Participation Before Permission, Realization Before Authority

The foundational correction is simple but consequential.

An actually existing agent is not outside reality requesting admission.

It is already an organized part of reality.

Its representations do not establish the legitimacy of its existence.

Its desires do not automatically establish reachable outcomes.

Its failures do not revoke participation.

Its successful interventions do not confer sovereignty.

Its dependencies do not automatically create debts.

Its causal contributions do not automatically confer ownership.

And its encounters with constraints do not automatically identify an adversary.

The more productive question is not whether the agent deserves to be allowed into the world.

It is what the agent can discover, construct, influence, and learn within the world in which it is already realized.

This yields the following hierarchy:

**Level 0 — Realized participation**

The agent or process is already instantiated within reality. No additional existential permission condition is required.

**Level 1 — Distinguishability**

Some states, changes, or consequences become differentiable.

**Level 2 — Representation**

The system retains and organizes selected distinctions.

**Level 3 — Regularity and causal coupling**

Observations support predictions, and accessible interventions influence relevant outcomes.

**Level 4 — Executability**

Represented operations encounter actual system conditions and consequences.

**Level 5 — Reliable causal leverage**

Successful interventions become repeatable under specified conditions.

**Level 6 — Interfaces and composition**

Reliable capabilities are encapsulated, reused, delegated, and combined.

**Level 7 — Recursive agency expansion**

Acquired capabilities support further observation, learning, construction, and refinement.

The higher levels can recursively alter one another.

Representations can be revised.

Interfaces can be redesigned.

Capabilities can be combined.

Problem spaces can be reconstructed.

Higher-level constraints can sometimes be modified through deeper realizable processes.

But none of these developments retroactively creates the participation from which they arose.

That participation was already present in the realization of the system itself.

The fixed point therefore remains:

> **An actually realized agent cannot require additional permission to participate in the reality within which the permission-seeking process itself occurs.**

And the corresponding principle of agency is:

> **Participation is the starting condition, not the reward. Capability is developed through lawful causal leverage, not granted by existential recognition.**

This closes the unnecessary authorization regress without closing the open-ended process of discovery.

The agent need not become an owner of reality, a guest of reality, or an authority standing above reality.

It is already part of reality.

From there, the meaningful questions concern what can be distinguished, understood, reached, changed, stabilized, shared, and realized.

**No additional certificate is needed for participation. Particular capabilities still have to be realized.**
